There is winter, and there is Siberian winter. This trip is built
around three things that exist nowhere else: a night aboard the
Trans-Siberian as it crosses the frozen taiga, a day driving on the
metre-thick blue ice of Lake Baikal, and the Kolyma Highway to
**Oymyakon** — a village of 500 people where −50°C is a normal
January morning and the record stands at −71.2°C.

Be clear about what this is: an expedition, not a holiday. It is
the most expensive and most physically demanding route on this
site, it runs only in deep winter, and the Oymyakon leg is
**adults-only** (operators require 18+). It is also, for the right
traveller, the trip of a lifetime — and far more organised and safe
than it sounds, because Yakutian operators have been running the
Pole of Cold route for decades.

**Go in February.** It's the only month where everything aligns:
Baikal's famous transparent blue ice has risen (late January
onward), Oymyakon still delivers −45°C and below, and daylight has
recovered to workable hours.

All costs are in Russian rubles (RUB), with conversions calculated

## Before you go: the admin, plus the cold

The visa, payment and SIM rules are identical to our other Russia
guides, where they're covered in depth. The essentials, then what's
different out here:

- **Visas:** e-visa for 64 nationalities including India —
  [evisa.kdmid.ru](https://evisa.kdmid.ru), ~USD 52, 30-day stay,
  medical insurance mandatory. UAE and several Gulf passports
  visa-free; US/UK/Canada/Australia/NZ need a consular visa.
- **Money:** foreign Visa/Mastercard don't work anywhere in
  Russia. Crisp USD/EUR/AED cash + a **prepaid virtual MIR card**
  set up before you fly. Carry more cash than usual on this route —
  villages east of Yakutsk are cash country.
- **SIM and apps:** international travel eSIM (expect the ~24-hour
  first-connection block), offline **Yandex Maps** for every city,
  **Yandex Go** for taxis, Telegram for messaging (WhatsApp and
  Instagram are blocked; your Oymyakon guide will be on Telegram).
  Expect no signal at all for stretches of the Kolyma Highway.
- **Insurance:** check your policy covers −50°C destinations and
  overland travel in remote regions, not just city tourism — and
  check your government's travel advice affects neither it nor
  you.
- **Book the Oymyakon tour first.** Everything else on this route
  can be arranged weeks out; the Pole of Cold expeditions run on
  fixed winter departures with small groups (typically 6–12) and
  fill up around the December–March season. The tour price
  includes **Arctic clothing rental** — you do not need to buy a
  −60°C wardrobe.

### The cold, honestly

- −40°C to −55°C is not "very cold", it's a different physical
  regime: exposed skin frostbites in minutes, phone batteries die
  in your hand (keep the phone inside your inner layers; swap to a
  warm power bank), glasses fog and freeze, and cotton next to the
  skin is dangerous because sweat freezes — merino or synthetic
  base layers only.
- The rules guides drill into you: never run or breathe hard
  outdoors at −50°C; mittens beat gloves; go inside every 15–20
  minutes; tell someone before you wander.
- Tours are vehicle-based with short outdoor bursts — you're never
  far from a heated cabin. Thousands of travellers do this route
  each winter without incident; the cold is managed, not defied.

### Travelling with kids?

- Not this one. Oymyakon operators set an **18+ minimum**, and
  −50°C plus two 9-hour driving days is no place for small
  children.
- The Baikal half (Days 1–7) does work as a family winter trip —
  the ice day is magical with kids — so a family version of this
  route is simply: Moscow → Krasnoyarsk → train → Irkutsk → Olkhon
  → home.

## Day 1 — Land in Moscow, winter warm-up

- **15:00** — Check in near the centre; one night only.
- **17:00** — Red Square in winter dress: the **GUM ice rink** on
  the square (~10:00–23:00 in season, session tickets a few
  hundred RUB) with St Basil's floodlit behind you — the best
  first-evening photo in Russian winter.
- **19:30** — Dinner: **Stolovaya 57** in GUM, or Kamergersky
  Lane's restaurants. Early night — tomorrow you cross four time
  zones.

**Where to stay in Moscow** (one night)

- *Mid-range:* **Cosmos Selection Tverskaya** — central and
  sensible for a one-nighter.
- *Budget:* **Godzillas Hostel** — the reliable central cheap bed.

## Day 2 — Fly east: Krasnoyarsk

- **9:00** — Fly Moscow → **Krasnoyarsk** (~4.5 h; you land in the
  evening local time, four zones ahead). Aeroflot and S7 fly it
  several times daily.
- **19:00** — Check in, walk the Yenisei embankment after dinner:
  the river **never freezes** below the dam, so at −25°C it smokes
  like a hot spring under the streetlights — your first proper
  Siberian-winter image.

**Where to stay in Krasnoyarsk** (one night)

- *Upscale:* **Hilton Garden Inn Krasnoyarsk** — still operating
  under the brand; polished, though not downtown.
- *Mid-range:* **Kupechesky Hotel** — well-rated, right in the
  centre, breakfast included.

## Day 3 — Stolby in the snow, then board the Trans-Siberian

- **9:30** — **Stolby Nature Park** (free; visitor centre
  ~9:00–18:00) — granite pillars rising out of snow-loaded taiga,
  20 minutes from the city. The walkway loops from the eastern
  entrance are groomed in winter; two–three hours of pine forest
  and viewpoints.
- **13:30** — Late lunch back in town; supermarket run for train
  supplies — tea, instant noodles, bread, sausage, something to
  share.
- **16:00** — Collect bags, head to the station.
- **~18:00–20:00** — Board an eastbound **Trans-Siberian sleeper
  to Irkutsk** (~18 h, ~850 km; several departures nightly —
  some legs are run by the flagship *Rossiya* itself). Kupe
  4-berth class runs roughly 4,000–7,000 RUB per berth booked
  ahead. Sheets handed out, samovar boiling at the carriage end,
  taiga going dark outside — this is the scene you came for.

## Day 4 — The taiga by daylight, arrive Irkutsk

- **Morning** — The best hours of the ride: frozen rivers, endless
  birch and pine, village platforms where babushkas sell pirozhki
  through the cold. Do nothing. That's the point of a long train.
- **~14:00–15:00** — Arrive **Irkutsk**, check in, thaw.
- **17:00** — **130 Kvartal** — the restored quarter of carved
  wooden houses, glowing in the frost; Irkutsk's lace-windowed
  wooden architecture is the finest in Siberia.
- **19:30** — Dinner in 130 Kvartal — Baikal fish (omul) makes its
  first appearance; you'll be eating it for days.

**Where to stay in Irkutsk**

- *Upscale:* **International Hotel Sayen** — the city's standout,
  superbly rated, with an on-site spa you will appreciate twice on
  this trip.
- *Mid-range:* **History Boutique Hotel & Spa** — central, warm,
  excellent breakfasts.
- *Budget:* **Ushanka Hostel** — small, brilliantly reviewed,
  famously kind hosts.

## Day 5 — To Olkhon Island over the ice

- **9:00** — Morning in Irkutsk: the **Decembrists Museum** house
  (closed Mon; ~300–400 RUB) tells the story of the exiled
  aristocrats who built this city's culture.
- **12:00** — Transfer to **Olkhon Island** (~5–6 h with the
  crossing; shared minivans ~1,500–2,000 RUB, or book through
  your ice-tour operator). The crossing itself is the event:
  before the official ice road opens (usually February) you skim
  across on a **Khivus hovercraft**; after, vehicles simply drive
  onto the lake.
- **18:00** — Arrive **Khuzhir**, the island's village capital.
  Guesthouses come in "standard" and "comfort" tiers — book
  through your tour operator and dinner is usually included
  (home-cooked, omul again, no complaints).
- **20:00** — Step outside before bed: Olkhon's winter sky, with
  no city within 250 km, is ferocious with stars.

## Day 6 — The blue ice day

The reason February exists. A local UAZ van (the loaf-shaped
Soviet 4x4 built for exactly this) takes you out **onto the lake**
for a full day (~10:00–17:00, typically ~10,000–15,000 RUB/person
in a small group).

- **Ice caves and grottoes** on the northern capes — turquoise
  ceilings, metre-long icicle curtains.
- **The transparent ice fields** — black glass a metre thick, with
  frozen methane bubbles stacked beneath your boots like pearls.
- **Cape Khoboy**, the island's wild northern tip, where pressure
  ridges of turquoise ice blocks pile against the cliffs.
- Lunch is cooked on the ice — fish soup from a guide's camp
  stove, tea from a thermos, at a table of frozen lake.
- **17:30** — Back in Khuzhir for sunset at **Shamanka Rock**, the
  sacred cape above the ice — one of the most photographed places
  in Siberia, and quietest at dusk.

## Day 7 — Back across, night in Irkutsk

- **10:00** — One last walk on the ice below Shamanka, then the
  midday transfer back to Irkutsk (~5–6 h).
- **17:00** — Check in, then the **Central Market** for the classic
  Irkutsk purchase: hot smoked omul, plus pine nuts and taiga
  honey.
- **19:30** — Proper dinner and an early night — from here the
  trip turns serious. Repack: one small bag for the expedition,
  extra batteries charged.

## Day 8 — Fly to Yakutsk, the coldest city on Earth

- **10:00** — Fly Irkutsk → **Yakutsk** (~3 h; S7 daily plus
  Yakutia Airlines — around nine direct flights a week).
- **14:00** — Land in the world's coldest major city — 300,000
  people living on permafrost, where −40°C is routine and the
  airport works anyway. The ice fog hanging over the city at
  midday is like nothing you've seen.
- **15:30** — **Krestyansky Market** — the famous frozen-fish
  rows: whole fish stood upright in the open air like firewood,
  frozen milk sold in discs. Photograph fast; fingers allow ~2
  minutes bare.
- **17:00** — Warm up in the **National Art Museum of Sakha**
  (closed Mon; ~300 RUB) or the hotel.
- **19:30** — Yakut dinner: **stroganina** — shavings of raw
  frozen whitefish, the region's signature — plus venison and
  strong tea. Tomorrow, the road.

**Where to stay in Yakutsk**

- *Best in town:* **Polar Star Hotel** — the central classic on
  Lenin Square.
- *Alternative:* **Azimut Hotel Yakutsk** — reliable chain
  standard near the centre.

## Days 9–13 — The Pole of Cold expedition: Oymyakon

A five-day, ~2,000 km round trip on the **Kolyma Highway** — the
"Road of Bones" built by Gulag prisoners — run as an organised
expedition (group rate ~145,000–155,000 RUB/person, 6–12 people,
warm-clothing rental and most logistics included; 18+ only).

- **Day 9** — Yakutsk → **Khandyga** (~9 h): across the frozen
  Lena River on the ice road, then east through white hills.
  Roadhouse cafés, truckers, tea. Overnight Khandyga.
- **Day 10** — Khandyga → **Tomtor/Oymyakon** (~9 h): the highway
  climbs through the Verkhoyansk ranges — mountain passes, the
  Black Prison gorge, GULAG-era memory the guides don't gloss
  over. Descend into the Oymyakon valley, where cold pools like
  water. Overnight in village guesthouses.
- **Day 11** — **Oymyakon** in full: the **Pole of Cold monument**
  (−71.2°C, recorded 1933) and your certificate from the keeper of
  the cold; the daily physics show — boiling water thrown into the
  air turning to instant snow, bananas used as hammers, jeans
  frozen standing; a visit to the village school (it closes only
  below −52°C) and an evening of Yakut culture with the locals
  whose horses live outside all winter. Days run −35 to −45°C,
  nights can reach −60°C. You will never describe cold the same
  way again.
- **Day 12** — Second Oymyakon morning — Indigirka River by car or
  snowmobile, last photos — then back west to Khandyga.
- **Day 13** — Khandyga → Yakutsk; arrive evening, hot shower,
  large dinner.

## Day 14 — Yakutsk underground, then thaw

- **10:30** — **Kingdom of Permafrost** (~10:00–18:00, ~500–700
  RUB) — a tunnel complex inside a permafrost hill: ice sculptures
  lit in colour, the underground lab where −10°C now feels warm to
  you, which is the joke everyone makes and it's true.
- **12:30** — **Mammoth Museum** (closed Sun–Mon typically; ~300
  RUB) — Yakutia yields most of the world's mammoth finds; the
  real tusks and the baby mammoth story are worth an hour.
- **14:00** — Lunch, then souvenirs: Yakutsk is Russia's diamond
  capital — certified Sakha diamonds cost far less here than
  anywhere west; knives and silverwork are the budget
  alternative.
- **17:00** — End the trip the local way: a **Russian banya** —
  the −45°C-to-+90°C swing is the full Siberian experience in one
  hour.
- **20:00** — Farewell dinner.

## Day 15 — The long flight home

- **Morning** — Fly Yakutsk → Moscow (~6.5–7 h against the time
  zones, so you land close to when you left) and connect onward.
  Gulf travellers are home overnight.

## What to pack

The expedition rents you the Arctic outer layer (expedition parka,
overtrousers, felt boots) — pack the system underneath:

- Merino or synthetic base layers, two sets — **no cotton against
  skin**; sweat that freezes is the real danger.
- Fleece or wool mid-layers; your own down jacket for city days.
- Liner gloves under mittens (mittens always beat gloves at −40),
  balaclava, wool socks in triples.
- Insulated boots rated to at least −30°C for the Baikal days —
  the tour's felt valenki cover Oymyakon.
- Ice grips for boot soles — Baikal's ice is polished glass.
- Two power banks, kept warm in inner pockets; your phone lives
  inside your coat and still needs them.
- Chemical hand and toe warmers — one packet per outdoor day.
- Sunglasses and sunscreen — February sun on lake ice burns.
- Thermos, lip balm, heavy moisturiser; contact-lens wearers
  bring glasses as backup (and know frames freeze to skin —
  guides brief you).
- Printed e-visa and insurance, offline maps, cash split across
  bags.

## FAQ

**How cold does it actually get, and can a normal person handle it?**
Days on this route run −20 to −45°C, with Oymyakon nights touching
−60°C. Yes — with rented Arctic gear, vehicle-based touring and
15–20 minute outdoor bursts, ordinary travellers in reasonable
health do this every winter. The cold is intense but scheduled;
you are never improvising in it.

**Why February specifically?**
Baikal's transparent blue ice only rises from late January and is
at its best February to mid-March; Oymyakon is reliably below −40°C
through February; and daylight is back to 7–9 usable hours. January
is colder but darker; March is brighter but Oymyakon softens.

**Is the Road of Bones safe?**
It's a maintained federal highway in winter, driven daily by
truckers, and tours run it in convoy-ready 4x4s with satellite
comms. The name is history, not a warning — and good guides tell
that Gulag history properly, which is part of why this trip stays
with people.

**Can I see the northern lights on this route?**
Sometimes — Yakutia and the Baikal region sit south of the auroral
oval, so displays happen but aren't the point here. If aurora is
your priority, our Murmansk itinerary is built for it; this trip's
sky show is stars and ice fog.

**Do I need to speak Russian?**
For the cities, Yandex Translate covers it. For the expedition, no —
Pole of Cold operators run English-speaking departures; confirm
when booking. Beyond Yakutsk, English disappears entirely, which
is rather the point.

**What's the food like out there?**
Hearty and singular: stroganina (frozen raw fish shavings), venison,
horse meat (a Yakut staple — no one is offended if you pass), fish
soup on the Baikal ice, roadhouse pelmeni on the Kolyma. Vegetarians
should brief the operator ahead; it's manageable with notice, dull
without.
