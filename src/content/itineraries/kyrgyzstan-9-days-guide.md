Kyrgyzstan is the most mountainous country most people have never
considered. Ninety-odd per cent of it sits above 1,500 m, the Tian
Shan runs across the whole of it, and the summer pastures still fill
with families who take their animals up and live in yurts until
autumn.

That last part is the trip. **Song-Kul** is an alpine lake at
3,016 m where you sleep in a yurt on a plain with no road, no
signal and an enormous sky. **Issyk-Kul** is the world's
second-largest alpine lake, warm enough to swim in, ringed by
snow peaks. **Karakol** has a wooden mosque built without nails
and a Russian Orthodox cathedral, both in the same small town.

Two things decide whether this works: **the season**, which is
short and non-negotiable, and **accepting that transport here is
shared taxis and dirt roads** rather than timetables.

## Before you go: the season, the roads and the yurts

### Entry, and why it is easy

- **Kyrgyzstan is one of the most open countries in the region.**
  Around sixty nationalities enter **visa-free for 60 days** — the
  EU, UK, US, Canada, Australia, Japan, Korea and more. Most others
  can use a straightforward **eVisa**.
- Passports outside that list, Indian among them, currently need an
  eVisa; it is applied for
  online and issued in a few working days. Check your own case,
  which is the fastest-changing detail here.
- Passport valid six months.

### The season is short, and it is the whole plan

- **The high pastures are open roughly late June to early
  September.** That is it.
- **Song-Kul is snowbound outside that window**, the yurt camps come
  down, and the passes close. Arriving in May or October means a
  different, lesser trip.
- **July and August** are the reliable months: warm days, cold
  nights, everything open.
- **Nights at Song-Kul are near freezing even in July.** The camps
  provide thick blankets and a stove; bring a warm layer regardless.

### How the country actually moves

- **Shared taxis (*marshrutka* and shared cars)** run between towns
  from set stands, leaving when full rather than on a timetable.
  They are cheap, frequent on main routes and how everyone travels.
- **For Song-Kul you need a driver.** The road is unpaved, steep and
  unsigned. Arrange a car with driver through your guesthouse — this
  is normal, expected and inexpensive.
- **A rented 4x4 is possible** if you are confident on mountain
  dirt, but a local driver costs little and knows which fords are
  passable this week.
- **Allow more time than distances suggest.** Bishkek to Song-Kul is
  around six hours for 300 km.

### Yurts, and what to expect

- **A yurt stay is the point of coming**, and it is simple rather
  than luxurious: felt walls, a stove, shared long-drop toilets,
  a wash basin, and meals with the family.
- **No electricity in most camps**, limited or no signal, and no
  hot shower. Bring a power bank and let people at home know.
- **Book through your guesthouse in Bishkek or Kochkor**, which is
  how it is done — the camps are family operations, not online
  businesses.
- **Community-based tourism organisations** in Kochkor and Karakol
  arrange yurt stays, horse treks and drivers, and the money stays
  local.

### Money and small things

- **The som (KGS).** Cards work in Bishkek and larger hotels and
  nowhere else on this route. **Withdraw cash in Bishkek** and carry
  enough for the whole mountain section.
- **No ATM at Song-Kul**, obviously, and often none in villages.
- Russian is the lingua franca; Kyrgyz is the national language.
  English is limited outside guesthouses.
- Tap water is not drinkable; the guesthouses provide boiled or
  bottled.

## Day 1 — Land in Bishkek

- **Afternoon** — Land, drop bags. Bishkek is a wide, green Soviet
  grid city with good coffee and no particular sights.
- **Evening** — **Ala-Too Square**, the changing of the guard, and
  dinner. Arrange your onward driver tonight.

## Day 2 — Burana, then south to Kochkor

- **Morning** — **Burana Tower**, an eleventh-century minaret on the
  Silk Road plain, with stone grave markers around it.
- **Afternoon** — Drive to **Kochkor**, the staging town for
  Song-Kul, about three hours.
- **Evening** — A guesthouse, a felt-making demonstration if you
  want one, and an early night.

## Day 3 — Up to Song-Kul

- **Morning** — The climb: three to four hours on dirt over the
  **Kalmak-Ashuu pass**, with the lake appearing suddenly on a
  plain at the top.
- **Afternoon** — Walk, or ride. The plain is enormous, the herds
  are everywhere, and there is nothing to do, which is the
  attraction.
- **Evening** — Dinner in the yurt with the family, and a sky with
  no light pollution for hundreds of kilometres.

## Day 4 — Song-Kul, and down the other side

- **Morning** — A horse ride along the shore, which is how the
  distances here are meant to be covered.
- **Afternoon** — Down the northern pass toward **Kochkor** and on
  east to the south shore of **Issyk-Kul**.
- **Evening** — Arrive at the lake.

## Day 5 — The canyons of the south shore

- **Morning** — **Skazka (Fairytale) Canyon**, eroded red and orange
  rock formations you walk into and over.
- **Afternoon** — **Jeti-Ögüz** — the Seven Bulls — red sandstone
  cliffs and a broken heart-shaped rock, with a valley behind them.
- **Evening** — On to **Karakol**.

## Day 6 — Karakol

- **Morning** — The **Dungan Mosque**, built by Chinese Muslim
  craftsmen in the style of a pagoda and without a single nail, and
  the wooden **Orthodox cathedral** built the same way.
- **Afternoon** — The **animal market** if it is Sunday, which is
  one of the best mornings in Central Asia.
- **Evening** — Ashlyan-fu, the local cold noodle dish, from a
  market stall.

## Day 7 — Ala-Kul or the valley

- **Option A** — A day hike up the **Karakol valley** toward
  Ala-Kul, which is a serious multi-day trek in full but gives a
  fine day walk from the trailhead.
- **Option B** — **Altyn-Arashan** by 4x4, a valley of hot springs
  under the peaks.
- **Evening** — Back in Karakol.

## Day 8 — The north shore back to Bishkek

- **Morning** — West along the **north shore** of Issyk-Kul, with a
  swim stop; the lake is slightly saline and never freezes.
- **Afternoon** — **Cholpon-Ata**'s petroglyph field, then on to
  Bishkek, about five hours in total.
- **Evening** — Last dinner in the city.

## Day 9 — Fly home

- Manas airport is 30 minutes out. Many flights leave in the very
  early hours; check yours before planning the last evening.

## What to pack

- **A warm layer and a hat for the yurt night**, even in July. It
  drops close to freezing at 3,000 m.
- **Walking boots** — the ground everywhere is uneven.
- **A power bank.** No electricity at Song-Kul.
- **Cash in som**, withdrawn in Bishkek, for the whole mountain leg.
- **Type C/F European plugs.**
- **Sun protection.** Altitude sun is fierce even when the air is
  cold.
- **Wet wipes and a quick-dry towel** for the days without a shower.

## FAQ

**Is the yurt stay comfortable?**
Simple rather than comfortable, and that is the deal: felt walls, a
stove, thick blankets, a long-drop toilet and no shower. It is also
the thing people remember for years.

**Can I go outside July and August?**
You can visit the country, but not this itinerary. Song-Kul and the
high pastures are closed by snow outside roughly late June to early
September.

**Do I need a guide?**
No, but you need drivers for the mountain sections, and the
community tourism offices in Kochkor and Karakol arrange those
cheaply and well.

**How does it compare to Kazakhstan?**
Kazakhstan is the easier, more urban trip — Almaty is a proper city
with mountains behind it. Kyrgyzstan is rougher, higher and more
remote, and the yurt nights have no equivalent there. See our
[Kazakhstan guide](/itineraries/kazakhstan-7-days-guide/) for the
other half of Central Asia.
