Cuba is the trip that most rewards preparation, because almost
nothing about it works the way travel usually does. **Your cards
will probably not work. The internet is limited. Shops are not
reliably stocked.** None of that spoils it — but arriving without
knowing it does.

What you get in return is a country unlike any other in the
hemisphere. **Havana** is a capital of collapsing baroque and 1950s
American cars where live music happens in ordinary bars rather than
for tourists. **Viñales** is a tobacco valley of limestone domes
farmed by ox and hand. **Trinidad** is a colonial sugar town so
completely preserved that the cobbles are the original ones.

Nine days covers all three at a Cuban pace, which is slower than you
plan for and better than you expect.

## Before you go: money, the card problem and the bookings

### Entry, and the tourist card

- **Most visitors need a tourist card** (*tarjeta del turista*) —
  effectively a visa, bought through your airline, a Cuban consulate
  or an agency, and valid for 30 days. It is usually straightforward
  and inexpensive; the airline selling you the flight will normally
  sell you the card.
- **Travel insurance is mandatory** and can be checked on arrival.
  Bring the policy document.
- **US travellers face separate restrictions** under their own
  country's rules, including permitted categories of travel and
  limits on where money may be spent. That is US law rather than
  Cuban, and it needs checking independently.
- **Visiting Cuba can affect later US entry** under the visa-waiver
  scheme — worth knowing if a US trip may follow.

### The money situation, which is the whole trip

This is the part that catches people, so it comes first.

- **Bring all the cash you need, for the entire trip, from home.**
  There is no reliable fallback.
- **Cards issued by US banks do not work in Cuba at all.** Cards
  from elsewhere work inconsistently at best — assume they will not.
- **Euros, and to a lesser extent pounds and Canadian dollars, are
  the practical currencies to bring.** US dollars are accepted in
  places but have at times attracted penalties on exchange; bring
  euros if you can.
- **Bring clean, undamaged notes.** Torn or marked ones get refused.
- **The official exchange rate and the rate people actually use are
  not the same**, and the gap has been large and volatile. Change
  money at your *casa*, a bank or a formal exchange, never on the
  street, and check the current situation close to travel.
- **This guide budgets in US dollars** because a figure in Cuban
  pesos would be meaningless by the time you read it.

### Stay in casas particulares

- **Casas particulares** — licensed rooms in private homes — are
  better than almost every hotel at the price, and the money goes to
  a family rather than a state chain.
- Your host will cook breakfast, arrange the next casa, book the
  taxi between towns and explain what is actually open this week.
  That last service is worth the stay on its own.
- Book the first two nights ahead and let your host chain the rest.

### Getting around, and what to expect

- **Shared and private taxis run between the towns**, arranged
  through your casa the day before. Viñales is about three hours
  from Havana, Trinidad about four and a half.
- **Viazul buses** are the cheaper scheduled option and need
  booking ahead.
- **Shortages are real and rotating.** Some weeks it is fuel, some
  weeks bottled water, some weeks eggs. Carry a refillable bottle
  with a filter, keep snacks, and treat any shop with what you need
  as the moment to buy it.
- **The internet is limited.** Wi-fi exists in hotels and some
  plazas via scratch cards; a Cuban tourist eSIM now works but not
  everywhere. Plan to be offline and tell people at home so.

## Day 1 — Land in Havana

- **14:00** — Land at **José Martí (HAV)**, 20 km south of the
  centre. There is no ride-hailing app: agree the taxi fare before
  you get in, in cash, and have small notes.
- **15:30** — Check into your **casa particular** in **Habana
  Vieja** or **Vedado**. Casas are private homes, licensed, and the
  single best decision on a Cuba trip — your hosts will solve
  problems no hotel desk can.
- **16:30** — Change money with your host or at a bank, never on
  the street. Bring **cash in euros**; cards issued by US banks do
  not work at all and others fail often enough that you should plan
  for none of them working.
- **18:00** — The **Malecón** at sunset. Eight kilometres of sea
  wall where the whole city comes to sit — fishermen, couples,
  trumpet practice, and the spray coming over when the wind is up.
- **20:30** — Dinner at the casa. Ask tonight about tomorrow's
  breakfast and the taxi west on day four; both want a day's notice.

## Day 2 — Old Havana on foot

- **09:00** — The four colonial plazas, in this order:
  **Plaza de la Catedral** for the baroque facade, **Plaza de
  Armas** and its second-hand book stalls, **Plaza de San
  Francisco** by the cruise dock, and **Plaza Vieja**, the
  liveliest, with its camera obscura for the rooftop view.
- **11:30** — The streets between them are the point rather than
  the squares — **Obispo** and **Mercaderes**, restoration on one
  block and propped-up ruins on the next.
- **13:00** — Lunch on a rooftop off Plaza Vieja.
- **15:00** — **Museo de la Revolución**, in the former Presidential
  Palace, with the *Granma* yacht outside under glass. It is the
  official version told with total confidence, and that is what
  makes it worth two hours.
- **17:30** — **El Floridita** or **La Bodeguita del Medio** if you
  want the Hemingway box ticked — both are crowded and overpriced,
  and one drink is the correct dose.
- **21:00** — Live **son** somewhere with no cover charge. Ask your
  host tonight, not a guidebook: the good rooms change month to
  month.

## Day 3 — The other Havana

- **09:30** — A **1950s American car tour** through Vedado and
  Miramar. It is touristy and unavoidable, and it is genuinely the
  best way to cover the wider city — convertible Chevrolets kept
  running for seventy years on improvised parts, which is its own
  story.
- **10:30** — **Plaza de la Revolución**: the José Martí memorial,
  and Che and Camilo picked out in steel on the ministry facades.
- **11:30** — **Fusterlandia** in Jaimanitas, where José Fuster has
  covered his own house and much of the neighbourhood in mosaic —
  Gaudí by way of the Caribbean, and still expanding.
- **13:30** — Lunch in Vedado.
- **15:00** — **Necrópolis de Colón**, 56 hectares of marble, or
  **Callejón de Hamel** for Afro-Cuban murals — the rumba there
  runs Sunday around noon and is a genuine community event rather
  than a performance.
- **17:30** — **Hotel Nacional** terrace for a daiquiri and the
  view down the Malecón. Cheaper than it looks.
- **19:30** — Dinner. Confirm the Viñales taxi for the morning.

## Day 4 — West to Viñales

- **09:00** — Three hours west by *colectivo* taxi, through
  increasingly green country and past roadside stalls selling
  strings of garlic and onions.
- **12:00** — Arrive **Viñales**. The valley is a UNESCO site of
  *mogotes* — limestone domes with vertical sides, left standing
  when the ground between them dissolved.
- **13:00** — Lunch at your casa.
- **15:00** — Walk or ride into the valley. On foot, the loop out
  past the tobacco fields and back takes two to three hours; on
  horseback it covers more and costs little.
- **17:30** — A *mirador* for sunset — the **Hotel Los Jazmines**
  terrace has the classic view over the whole valley.
- **19:30** — Dinner at the casa, which will be better than any
  restaurant in town and roughly half the price.

## Day 5 — Tobacco, caves and horses

- **08:30** — A **tobacco farm**. The drying barns, the
  hand-rolling, and a straight answer about the arrangement: the
  state takes 90% of the crop at a fixed price, the farmer keeps
  10%, and what he rolls himself is what you are smoking.
- **10:30** — A **coffee** and honey smallholding, usually on the
  same circuit.
- **12:30** — Lunch.
- **14:00** — **Cueva del Indio**, entered on foot and left by boat
  along the underground river — twenty minutes, mildly kitsch, and
  everyone does it.
- **15:30** — The **Mural de la Prehistoria**, 120 m of painted
  cliff from 1961. It is not good art. It is a good story, and the
  cocktail terrace beneath it has the better view.
- **17:30** — Sunset from the valley floor, or a second ride.
- **19:30** — Dinner, and an early night — tomorrow is long.

## Day 6 — The long drive to Trinidad

- **07:00** — Leave early. Viñales to Trinidad is around seven
  hours by *colectivo*, skirting Havana and crossing the island.
- **11:00** — Most drivers stop at **Playa Girón** or the **Bay of
  Pigs** — the museum there tells the 1961 invasion from the Cuban
  side, and the snorkelling off the shore is genuinely good if you
  have brought a mask.
- **13:30** — Lunch at **Cienfuegos**, the French-founded city with
  a Parisian-looking main square, worth the 45 minutes you get.
- **17:00** — Arrive **Trinidad**, cobbled, low-rise and largely
  unchanged since the sugar money stopped in the 1860s.
- **20:00** — Dinner, then the **Casa de la Música** steps beside
  the church, where a band plays every night and the town turns up.

## Day 7 — Trinidad, and the sugar valley

- **08:30** — **Plaza Mayor** before the heat: the pastel palaces
  of the sugar barons, and the **Museo Histórico Municipal** whose
  **Cantero tower** gives the roof-level view over the whole town.
- **10:30** — The cobbled streets away from the square, which is
  where Trinidad stops being a museum and starts being a town.
- **12:30** — Lunch.
- **14:30** — The **Valle de los Ingenios**, the sugar valley
  worked by 30,000 enslaved people at its peak. The **Manaca
  Iznaga** tower, 45 m, was built to watch them; you climb it now
  for the view. The steam train runs some seasons and not others —
  a taxi covers it either way.
- **17:30** — Back for sunset from the **Ermita de Popa** ruins
  above town.
- **21:00** — The steps again, and the **Disco Ayala** in a cave
  above the town afterwards if you want the late version.

## Day 8 — Beach, then back

- **09:00** — **Playa Ancón**, twenty minutes south — white sand on
  the Caribbean, and the swimming half of the trip.
- **12:30** — Back for lunch, and collect your bags.
- **14:00** — Drive to Havana, about four and a half hours on the
  *autopista*.
- **18:30** — Arrive. Last casa, in Habana Vieja if you can.
- **20:30** — Dinner somewhere you liked the first time, then the
  Malecón. The city is at its best on the last night, when you
  have stopped trying to work it out.

## Day 9 — Fly home

- **Morning** — **Allow far more time than seems reasonable.**
  Cuban airports are slow, the queues do not move for your
  schedule, and three hours is not excessive.
- **Before you go** — Spend the last of your cash. Converting it
  back is difficult and carrying it out is pointless.
- **Note** — There is no reliable airport wi-fi. Tell people at
  home you will be uncontactable until you land.

## What to pack

- **All your money, in clean euro notes**, split between two places.
- **A refillable bottle with a filter**, because bottled water is
  one of the things that periodically runs out.
- **Basic medicines** — painkillers, rehydration salts, plasters,
  antihistamines. Pharmacies are poorly stocked, and small gifts of
  these to your hosts are genuinely appreciated.
- **Type A/B and C sockets both appear**; bring a universal adapter.
- **A torch.** Power cuts are common.
- **Cash-friendly small denominations** for tips and music.

## FAQ

**Will my card really not work?**
Assume not. US-issued cards definitely will not. Others are
unreliable. Bring cash for the whole trip, plus a margin.

**Is it safe?**
Cuba has very low violent crime and visitors are generally left
alone. Petty scams around tourist areas exist — inflated taxi fares,
"my cousin's restaurant" — and are the main annoyance.

**Hotel or casa particular?**
Casa, on every measure: better rooms at the price, far better food,
and a host who can explain what is open, what is short this week and
how to get to the next town.

**When should I go?**
**December to April** is the dry season and the best weather.
**June to November** is hurricane season, with September and October
the peak risk.
