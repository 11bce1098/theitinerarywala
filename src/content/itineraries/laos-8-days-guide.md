Every traveller who has done Thailand and Vietnam asks the same
question eventually, and Laos is the answer. It is the one country
in mainland Southeast Asia that never quite got busy: fewer people
than most single Thai provinces, a river that organises the whole
country, and a pace that is genuinely slower rather than marketed as
such.

**Luang Prabang** is the reason to come — a peninsula of French
colonial shophouses and thirty-odd gilded temples where every
morning at dawn, hundreds of monks walk the streets to collect alms.
**Vang Vieng** has spent a decade rebuilding its reputation from
river-tubing infamy into something much better: limestone karsts,
lagoons and balloons at sunrise. **Vientiane** is the most relaxed
capital in Asia, which is a low bar it clears easily.

The trip changed recently. The **Laos–China Railway**, opened in
2021, turned the grim nine-hour mountain bus between Luang Prabang
and Vang Vieng into a **two-hour train** — and made this route
genuinely comfortable for the first time.

## Before you go: visas, the train and the river

### Entry

- **Visa on arrival, or eVisa, for most nationalities.** Laos issues
  a 30-day visa on arrival at the main airports and land borders to
  most passports, and an **eVisa online** avoids the queue. Fees
  vary by nationality and are usually payable in **US dollars cash**
  at the counter.
- A small number of nationalities — most of ASEAN, Japan, Korea,
  Russia and a few others — enter visa-free. Check your own case.
- **Bring clean US dollar notes** for the visa fee and a passport
  photo; both are asked for and neither is reliably available on
  arrival.
- Passport valid six months, one blank page.

### The railway changed this trip

- **Book the train in advance.** Seats sell out, particularly in
  high season, and foreign bookings often need a passport at
  purchase. Hotels and agents book it routinely for a small fee.
- **Stations are well outside the towns** — allow 30–45 minutes and
  a shared van at each end. This is the part people miss and then
  miss the train.
- **Luang Prabang → Vang Vieng is about an hour**; **Vang Vieng →
  Vientiane about another hour**. The old bus route took most of a
  day.
- Minivans still run and cost less. The road is winding and the
  train is worth the difference.

### Money, and the zeros

- **The kip (LAK)**, and the numbers are large — prices run into the
  hundreds of thousands. Take a moment on arrival to fix a mental
  conversion, and expect to feel briefly wealthy.
- **Cash country.** Cards work at hotels and better restaurants in
  the three towns on this route and nowhere else. ATMs exist in all
  three but have low withdrawal limits and per-transaction fees.
- **US dollars and Thai baht are accepted** in tourist places, at a
  poor rate. Pay in kip.
- This is the **cheapest country on this site** by some distance —
  which is why the budget below looks alarming in kip and is not.

### The alms, and how to behave

- **The morning alms round is a religious observance, not a
  performance.** If you watch: stand well back on the opposite side
  of the street, stay silent, do not use flash, and do not block the
  monks' path.
- **Do not buy alms food from street vendors** to join in unless you
  understand what you are doing — poor-quality food handed to monks
  is a real and documented problem.
- **Dress modestly at temples**: shoulders and knees covered, shoes
  off before entering, and never point your feet at a Buddha image.
- Touching anyone's head, including children's, is impolite.

### When to go

- **November to February** is the dry, cool season and the best
  time — clear skies, comfortable days, cool nights.
- **March and April** bring heat and, in the north, agricultural
  burning that can leave the air genuinely poor for weeks.
- **May to October** is the wet season: green, lush, quiet, with
  waterfalls at their most dramatic and afternoon rain most days.

## Day 1 — Land in Luang Prabang

- **Afternoon** — Land, drop bags. The airport is ten minutes from
  town.
- **Evening** — Climb **Mount Phousi** for the sunset over the
  Mekong, then the **night market** along Sisavangvong Road, which
  is handicrafts rather than souvenir tat.

## Day 2 — Alms at dawn, temples after

- **05:30** — The **tak bat** alms procession, observed properly
  from a respectful distance.
- **Morning** — Back to sleep, or the **morning market** for
  breakfast.
- **Late morning** — **Wat Xieng Thong**, the finest temple in the
  country, and the old quarter's French-colonial streets.
- **Afternoon** — The **Royal Palace Museum**, then a Mekong sunset
  boat.

## Day 3 — Kuang Si waterfalls

- **Morning** — **Kuang Si**: turquoise pools stacked down a
  hillside, swimmable, and the bear sanctuary at the entrance.
  Go early; the tour groups arrive by eleven.
- **Afternoon** — Back to town. **Traditional Arts Centre** for the
  ethnic-textile collection, or a weaving village across the river.
- **Evening** — Dinner on the riverfront.

## Day 4 — Luang Prabang slow

- **Morning** — **Pak Ou caves** by boat — thousands of Buddha
  images in a limestone cavern above the Mekong — or a cooking class.
- **Afternoon** — Nothing scheduled. This is the town for it.
- **Evening** — Book tomorrow's train transfer.

## Day 5 — Train to Vang Vieng

- **Morning** — Van to the station, train south, about an hour.
- **Afternoon** — Arrive among the karsts. **Blue Lagoon 1** is the
  famous one and the busiest; **2 and 3** are quieter and better.
- **Evening** — Sunset from a riverside bar, watching the limestone
  go pink.

## Day 6 — Karsts, caves and the river

- **Sunrise** — **Hot-air balloon**, if the budget stretches. It is
  the image people bring home.
- **Morning** — **Tham Chang** cave, or kayaking the Nam Song.
- **Afternoon** — Tubing, which is now a gentle float rather than
  its former self.
- **Evening** — Early night.

## Day 7 — Train to Vientiane

- **Morning** — Train south, about an hour.
- **Afternoon** — **Pha That Luang**, the golden stupa that is the
  national symbol, and **Patuxai**, the victory arch built with
  cement donated for an airport runway.
- **Evening** — The **Mekong riverfront night market**, and dinner
  looking across at Thailand.

## Day 8 — Fly home

- **Morning** — **COPE Visitor Centre** if you have a few hours: a
  small, sobering museum about unexploded ordnance, and the most
  important thing in the city.
- Then out. Vientiane's airport is small and close.

## What to pack

- **Modest clothing for temples** — shoulders and knees, in both
  towns.
- **Slip-on shoes.** You will remove them constantly.
- **Cash in clean US dollars** for the visa, plus a card as backup.
- **Type A, B, C and F sockets all appear** — bring a universal
  adapter.
- **A warm layer** for November to February evenings, especially in
  Luang Prabang, which sits higher than you expect.
- **Insect repellent**; dengue is present year-round.

## FAQ

**Is Laos worth it if I have done Thailand and Vietnam?**
That is precisely when it is worth it. It offers the same landscapes
and food traditions at a fraction of the pace and the crowd, and
Luang Prabang has no equivalent in either country.

**Is the train really better than the bus?**
Yes, decisively. It converts a winding nine-hour mountain journey
into an hour, for a modest fare. Book ahead and allow time to reach
the stations, which are well out of town.

**Is it safe?**
On this route, yes. Laos is one of the region's calmer countries.
The genuine hazard is **unexploded ordnance** in rural areas from
the war — stay on marked paths outside towns, which is sound advice
here in a way it is not elsewhere.

**How many days do I need?**
Eight covers this route well. Add three if you want **Nong Khiaw** or
the **4,000 Islands** in the far south, which are long journeys from
this loop.
