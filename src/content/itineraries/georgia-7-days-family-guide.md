Georgia is the easiest "real mountains" trip most families will ever
plan: 3–4 hour flights from the Gulf and much of Europe, and prices
that make a full week cost less than a long weekend elsewhere. This
plan keeps drives under two hours on most days, which is the
difference between a holiday and a hostage situation when you travel
with a preschooler.

Everything below is written for a family of three (two adults, one
small child) travelling with a stroller. Costs are in Georgian lari
(GEL) with UAE dirham (AED) equivalents — at the time of writing,
1 GEL ≈ AED 1.35.

## Before you go: the 15-minute admin

### Do you need a visa?

Georgia is one of the most open countries in the world for tourists,
but the answer depends on your passport — and, for many readers of
this site, on your residence permit.

**Visa-free passports.** Citizens of the EU, UK, USA, GCC countries
(UAE, Saudi Arabia, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, Oman), and around 90
other countries enter visa-free for up to one full year. No forms,
no fees.

**Everyone else: e-visa.** If your passport is not on the visa-free
list, apply on the official portal at
[evisa.gov.ge](https://www.evisa.gov.ge) — around USD 20, fully
online, typically processed in 5 working days. Apply at least two
weeks before flying to be safe.

**Indian passport + UAE (or other Gulf/US/UK/Schengen) residence:**
this is the shortcut many expat families do not know about. If you
hold a valid residence permit from the UAE, another GCC state, the
US, UK, or a Schengen country, you can enter Georgia **visa-free for
up to 90 days** on an Indian passport — no e-visa needed. The
residence permit must be valid on arrival (carry the physical
Emirates ID and check your visa validity; officers do look). Rules
change, so confirm on the
[Georgian MFA site](https://mfa.gov.ge) close to your travel date.

### The new rule almost nobody knows yet: mandatory insurance

**Since 1 January 2026, Georgia requires all foreign visitors to
carry travel insurance** covering health and accidents for the full
length of stay, with minimum coverage of 30,000 GEL (roughly
USD 11,000). Border officers can ask for proof in paper or
electronic form, in English or Georgian.

In practice: buy a standard family travel policy before you fly
(most cost less than one dinner out), save the PDF on your phone,
and make sure the certificate shows the dates, the covered
territory including Georgia, and everyone's names. If you already
buy travel insurance anyway — and with a small child, you should —
this changes nothing except that you now need it at the border, not
just for peace of mind.

### Money

Georgia runs on the lari (GEL). Cards are accepted almost
everywhere in Tbilisi, but you will want cash for the cable car
top-up, mountain drivers, market stalls, and village cafés. Skip
the airport exchange counters: withdraw lari from a bank ATM in the
city (Bank of Georgia and TBC are everywhere), or exchange at the
small kiosks around the Old Town, which offer surprisingly fair
rates. Tipping is 10% and often added to the bill already — check
before adding more.

### SIM card and data

Do **not** buy the tourist SIM at Tbilisi Airport unless you truly
cannot wait — airport packages start around 40 GEL for 3 GB, while
the same operators in the city sell a SIM for 10 GEL and unlimited
30-day data for about 35 GEL. **Magti** has the best coverage,
including the mountain stretch to Kazbegi, and sells eSIMs too. Any
Magti shop in the city sets it up in five minutes with your
passport. If you want data the moment you land, a small
international eSIM for day one plus a Magti SIM in town is the
cheapest combination.

### Getting around

Inside Tbilisi, use **Bolt** — the ride-hailing app is everywhere,
cheap (most rides across town cost 5–15 GEL), and saves you from
negotiating with street taxis. Install it and add a card before you
fly. For everything outside the city, this itinerary uses one
private driver day and two organised day trips, which beats renting
a car: the Military Highway is spectacular but shared with fuel
trucks crossing to Russia, and Georgian mountain driving is not the
place to learn.

### The stroller reality check

Tbilisi's Old Town is cobblestones, hills, and the occasional
staircase. A lightweight, foldable travel stroller with decent
wheels works; a heavy pram fights you all week. Every stop on this
itinerary is doable with a stroller, and I flag the two places
where a carrier is genuinely the better tool.

## Day 1 — Land in Tbilisi, Old Town on foot

Ease in. Stay near the Old Town or the Sololaki/Rike side of the
river so everything this week starts from your doorstep.

The one must-do today: the **cable car from Rike Park up to
Narikala Fortress**. Kids love the ride, the view is the best in Tbilisi for the price
of a bus ticket, and queues are short early — it opens late morning, so go soon after opening or leave it
for the golden-hour light. Two things first-timers get wrong: the
fare (2.5 GEL each way) can only be paid with a **Metromoney
card** — no cash, no bank cards at the cabin — so buy the card at
the Rike station kiosk and load enough for the family both ways;
and wind closes the line temporarily, so if it is stormy, swap this
for Day 5's plan.

Up top, walk the fortress walls (carrier territory — leave the
stroller folded), then ride back down rather than walking the steep
path with a tired child. Finish at a courtyard restaurant in the
Old Town with khinkali (soup dumplings — order the fried ones for
kids, less scalding), khachapuri, and Georgian lemonade. Dinner for
three: 60–90 GEL.

## Day 2 — Mtskheta half-day trip

Georgia's ancient capital is 30 minutes from Tbilisi — the perfect
first excursion because it fits entirely inside a preschooler's
good-mood window.

Go up to **Jvari Monastery** first for the postcard view over the
meeting point of two rivers, then down into town for
**Svetitskhoveli Cathedral**, one of the holiest sites in Georgia
and impressively huge even to a four-year-old. The town centre
between the cathedral and the souvenir lanes is flat and
stroller-friendly. Shoulders and knees should be covered inside
churches; scarves are available at the entrances.

A Bolt or taxi there and back with waiting time costs around
80–100 GEL, or join a small-group half-day tour if you prefer a
guide. Either way, you are back in Tbilisi by nap time.

## Day 3 — Drive the Georgian Military Highway to Kazbegi

Today is the longest drive of the week — about three hours of
moving time — but it is also the best road trip in the country, and
the stops break it into 45–60 minute segments a small child can
absorb.

Book a **private driver** for this leg rather than the shared
minivans: you control the stops, the pace, and the nap logistics,
and it costs far less than you expect. The classic stops, in order:

**Ananuri Fortress** (1 hour in) — a 17th-century castle on a
turquoise reservoir. Twenty minutes of tower-climbing and photos is
plenty. **Gudauri viewpoints and the Russia–Georgia Friendship
Monument** — a huge circular mosaic balcony hanging over the
valley; often paragliders overhead, which is free entertainment for
kids. Then over the 2,395 m Jvari Pass and down to
**Stepantsminda** (the town everyone calls Kazbegi), where — on a
clear day — Mount Kazbek fills the window of your hotel room.

Choose a hotel with a mountain-view room and a restaurant on site;
after the drive, nobody wants to go out hunting for dinner.

## Day 4 — Gergeti Trinity Church, then deliberately nothing

The image you have seen of Georgia — a lone church on a green
ridge under a giant snow peak — is **Gergeti Trinity Church**, and
it sits directly above the town.

With small kids, skip the two-hour hike and take a local 4x4 from
the main square: drivers wait there all day, the ride up takes
15–20 minutes, and the view is identical. Expect around
30–50 GEL per person in a shared jeep or 100–150 GEL for a private
one; agree the price and the waiting time before you get in. Go
before 10:00 to beat the tour buses from Tbilisi. The final walk
from the parking to the church is short but uneven — carrier, not
stroller.

The afternoon is intentionally empty. This is the rest day that
makes the rest of the week work: playground on the village square,
a slow lunch of khachapuri and mountain honey, an early night.
If the peak was hidden in cloud this morning, you get a second
chance at sunrise tomorrow — Kazbek is clearest early.

## Day 5 — Back to Tbilisi via the Chronicle of Georgia

Retrace the highway south (the stops you skipped on the way up fill
the drive nicely), and just before entering Tbilisi, stop at the
**Chronicle of Georgia** — a hilltop crown of 30-metre carved
pillars overlooking the Tbilisi Sea. It looks like the set of a
fantasy film, has almost no crowds, and is the single most dramatic
photo stop of the trip. Flat access from the parking; fully
stroller-friendly.

If energy allows in the evening, ride the **funicular up to
Mtatsminda Park** — an amusement park on the mountain above the
city with rides sized for small children and the best night view of
Tbilisi. This is the reliable backup plan any time weather ruins
another day.

## Day 6 — Kakheti wine country day trip

East of Tbilisi lies Kakheti, the oldest wine region on earth, and
its showpiece town **Sighnaghi** — hilltop, pastel-coloured, ringed
by defensive walls with views across the Alazani Valley to the
Caucasus. The walk along the walls is level and stroller-friendly,
the town is compact, and the pace is slow in the best way.

Family day tours from Tbilisi typically include Sighnaghi, the
Bodbe Monastery gardens, and a family winery where adults taste
qvevri wine and kids get grape juice from the same vineyard. It
works better than it sounds. The drive is about 1h45 each way —
time both legs with naps and it disappears.

## Day 7 — Souvenirs and fly home

**Dry Bridge Market** in the morning: Soviet cameras, horn cups,
enamel badges, old carpets — the best souvenir hunting in the
Caucasus and a genuinely fun browse even with a child in tow. Grab
a final khachapuri, then head out. Evening departures get most Gulf
and European travellers home at a survivable bedtime.

## What to pack that you will actually use

A lightweight travel stroller and a soft carrier — you need both,
and the carrier wins at Narikala and Gergeti. Layers, even in
summer: Tbilisi can be 30°C while Kazbegi is 12°C and windy the
same afternoon. A rain shell for everyone; mountain weather turns
in minutes. Snacks for the drives — shops on the Military Highway
are sparse between Gudauri and Stepantsminda. A scarf for church
visits. Motion-sickness tablets if your child is prone; the
switchbacks before Jvari Pass are the one stretch that tests small
stomachs. And your insurance PDF, downloaded offline.

## FAQ

**Is Georgia safe for families?**
Yes — street crime is rare, locals adore children (expect your kid
to be gifted fruit and sweets by strangers), and the biggest
everyday risks are uneven pavements and enthusiastic driving. Use
Bolt, hold hands near roads, and it is one of the easiest countries
in the region to travel with kids.

**When is the best time to go?**
May–June and September–early October are ideal: warm in Tbilisi,
clear in the mountains, before or after the July–August peak.
Kazbegi is snowbound and harder to reach in deep winter; the
Gergeti road can close after heavy snowfall.

**Will my picky eater survive Georgian food?**
Easily. Khachapuri is cheese bread, khinkali are dumplings, lobiani
is bean-stuffed bread, and every menu has plain grilled chicken,
rice, and chips. Georgian lemonade (try the pear one) is the
national kids' drink.

**Do I need to rent a car?**
No — and with small kids I would not. Bolt covers Tbilisi, day
tours cover Kakheti and Mtskheta, and a private driver for the
Kazbegi leg costs less than two days of rental plus fuel plus
nerves.

**How far in advance should I book?**
Flights and the Kazbegi hotel: 4–8 weeks ahead in summer.
Everything else — tours, drivers, Tbilisi hotels — can be booked a
week out except in August.

**Can I do this itinerary in reverse?**
Yes, but landing into two slow Tbilisi days first lets everyone
find their feet before the mountain drive — with jet-lagged kids,
that ordering earns its keep.
