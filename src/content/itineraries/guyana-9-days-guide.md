**Kaieteur Falls** drops **226 metres in a single uninterrupted
plunge** — about five times the height of Niagara — carrying an
enormous volume of water off a sandstone plateau into a gorge.
It is the largest single-drop waterfall in the world by the
combination of height and flow.

What makes it extraordinary is not the statistic. It is that you
arrive by light aircraft on a grass strip, walk twenty minutes
through forest with a guide, and **stand at an unfenced edge with
perhaps a dozen other people on the entire plateau**. There is no
railing, no visitor centre, no queue. Compare that with Iguazú or
Victoria Falls and the difference is the whole point of Guyana.

The rest follows: **Iwokrama**, a million acres of rainforest run
as a research reserve with a canopy walkway in it; the **Rupununi**
savannah with ranches you stay on and giant anteaters in the
grass; and **Georgetown**, a Dutch-planned, British-built capital
of enormous wooden buildings below sea level.

This is **South America's least-visited mainland country**, and
it is not a cheap or easy one.

## Before you go: cost, logistics and the honest caveats

### Entry and health

- **Many nationalities enter visa-free**, including the UK, EU,
  US and Canada. **Check your own case** — the list is narrower
  than for neighbouring countries.
- **Yellow fever vaccination** is required and the certificate is
  checked.
- **Malaria is present in the interior**, particularly the
  Rupununi and the forest. Take prophylaxis.

### This is an expensive country to travel

- **Everything in the interior is reached by light aircraft or by
  a very long road.** The Georgetown–Lethem road is 14–16 hours in
  the dry season and impassable in the wet.
- **Kaieteur is almost always a day flight from Georgetown** —
  weather-dependent, and **it is cancelled regularly**. Build a
  spare day.
- **Lodges are all-inclusive** because there is no alternative
  within a hundred miles.
- Budget accordingly: this costs more than Brazil or Colombia for
  the same number of days, and the reason is aviation fuel.

### Georgetown, straight

- Georgetown has a **genuine crime problem** and the standard
  advice is to be careful. **Stay in a reputable hotel, use
  arranged transport rather than hailing taxis, and do not walk
  around after dark** or in the market areas with valuables.
- The city is worth a day for the architecture and the market. It
  is not a place to wander aimlessly.
- The interior — the lodges, the river and the savannah — is an
  entirely different matter and is very safe.

### When to go

- **Two dry seasons: mid-September to mid-November, and
  mid-February to late April.** These are the times to come.
- **Kaieteur is at its most spectacular in the wet** (May–August,
  December–January) when the volume is enormous — but flights are
  cancelled far more often and the roads close.
- **The Rupununi floods** in the wet season and becomes a
  different landscape reached by boat.
- **Late September to October** is probably the best compromise.

### Money and language

- **Guyanese dollar (GYD)**, and the notes carry large numbers.
  **US dollars are widely accepted** and often preferred for
  tours.
- **Cash for the interior** — there is nothing to draw it from
  once you leave Georgetown.
- **English is the official language**, the only South American
  country where it is — a British colonial inheritance, along with
  cricket, which matters enormously here.

## Day 1 — Land in Georgetown

- **Most international flights land late at night** at **Cheddi
  Jagan (GEO)**, which is **40 km south of the city** — about an
  hour, and arrange the transfer with your hotel rather than
  finding one at arrivals.
- **09:00** — Having slept: **Stabroek Market**, a cast-iron
  Victorian hall of 1881 under a clock tower, selling everything,
  and chaotic in a way that is worth seeing. **Leave valuables at
  the hotel and go with a guide or driver.**
- **11:00** — **St George's Cathedral**, which at 43 m is **among
  the tallest wooden buildings in the world**, built in 1892
  entirely of greenheart timber.
- **12:30** — Lunch, then the **seawall** — **Georgetown sits
  below sea level** and is held back by a Dutch-built defence that
  runs for kilometres, with the whole city walking along it at
  dusk.
- **15:00** — The **Botanical Gardens** and the **manatees** in
  the ponds there, which are tame enough to hand-feed grass.
- **17:00** — The **Promenade Gardens** and the wooden colonial
  houses on Main Street, built on stilts with Demerara shutters.
- **19:00** — Dinner. Guyanese food is the mix the country is:
  **pepperpot** (an Amerindian dish of cassareep-stewed meat),
  curry and roti from the Indian majority, Chinese, and Creole.
- **20:30** — **Confirm the Kaieteur flight** and the interior
  legs.

## Day 2 — Kaieteur Falls

- **07:00** — To the domestic terminal at **Ogle**. The day's
  flights go when the weather allows, so there may be a wait.
- **08:30** — **The flight in is an hour** over unbroken
  rainforest in a small aircraft — **the canopy runs to the
  horizon in every direction with no road, no clearing and no
  smoke**, and that view alone justifies the ticket.
- **09:30** — Land on a grass strip at the top of the plateau. A
  ranger meets the plane.
- **10:00** — Walk twenty minutes through forest to the first
  viewpoint, then a second, then a third — **each closer to the
  lip, each unfenced**. At the last you stand at the edge of a
  **226 m drop** with the Potaro River going over beside you and
  spray coming back up.
- **10:30** — Look for the **golden rock frog**, which lives its
  entire life in the water held by giant tank bromeliads on the
  plateau and is found nowhere else, and the **Guianan cock-of-
  the-rock**, orange and absurd, in the forest on the walk.
- **11:30** — **Swifts** nest behind the water and fly through the
  falls at dusk to roost, which you will probably not see on a day
  trip and is worth knowing about.
- **12:30** — Fly back, often with a stop at **Orinduik Falls** on
  the Brazilian border — a series of wide stepped cascades over
  jasper where you can swim.
- **16:00** — Georgetown.
- **If it was cancelled** — this is why you keep a spare day.

## Day 3 — South to Iwokrama

- **07:00** — **Fly to Fair View or Annai**, about an hour, or
  take the road if you have a full day and a strong constitution.
- **08:30** — **Iwokrama**: **371,000 hectares of rainforest**
  given by Guyana to the international community in 1989 as a
  living experiment in whether a forest can be used sustainably
  and still function. It is run jointly with the Amerindian
  communities who live in it.
- **10:00** — Settle at **Iwokrama River Lodge** on the
  **Essequibo** — Guyana's great river, a kilometre wide here.
- **12:00** — Lunch, then the heat.
- **15:30** — A **river trip**: **black caiman** — the largest
  predator in the Amazon basin, up to five metres — are studied
  here and seen regularly, plus river otters and dozens of bird
  species on the banks.
- **18:30** — **Spotlighting on the water** after dark for caiman
  eyeshine, which is unnerving in a small boat.
- **20:00** — Dinner at the lodge, and the forest at night is
  extremely loud.

## Day 4 — The canopy walkway

- **05:30** — Out early for the **Iwokrama Canopy Walkway**: a
  series of suspension bridges and platforms **30 m up in the
  canopy**, 154 m long, and you are at the level where the birds
  and monkeys actually live.
- **06:00** — Dawn from the platforms is the point — **toucans,
  macaws, spider monkeys and howlers**, and the forest waking up
  underneath you.
- **08:30** — Breakfast at Atta Lodge.
- **10:30** — A **forest walk** with an Amerindian guide, which is
  where you learn what every plant is for. **Iwokrama has the
  highest recorded density of jaguar in the world** and sightings
  on the forest road are a genuine possibility — rare, but people
  do see them here.
- **13:00** — Lunch, rest.
- **16:00** — **Turtle Mountain** if you have the legs: a 90-minute
  climb through forest to a bare summit with the canopy stretching
  unbroken to the horizon on all sides.
- **19:00** — Dinner.

## Day 5 — To the Rupununi

- **07:30** — South out of the forest, and within an hour the
  landscape changes completely: the trees stop and the **Rupununi
  savannah** begins — open grassland with termite mounds, scattered
  palms and mountains on the horizon.
- **10:00** — **Annai** and the Makushi villages at the forest
  edge.
- **12:00** — Arrive at a ranch — **Karanambu**, **Rock View** or
  **Caiman House**, depending on your route.
- **13:00** — Lunch, then the afternoon off.
- **16:00** — **Karanambu** was Diane McTurk's ranch, where she
  rehabilitated **giant river otters** for decades — they are up to
  1.8 m long and the work continues.
- **17:30** — Out on the river or the savannah at dusk for
  **giant anteaters**, which are most active in the cool and are
  one of the most reliable sightings here.
- **19:30** — Dinner at the ranch table with whoever else is
  staying, which will be four people.

## Day 6 — The savannah and the lily

- **05:30** — Out at first light. The Rupununi holds **giant
  anteater, capybara, black caiman, and over 400 bird species** in
  open country where you can actually see them.
- **08:00** — Breakfast.
- **10:00** — A **Makushi or Wapishana village** visit, arranged
  through the ranch — cassava processing, the bitter kind that
  must be pressed to remove the cyanide, which is the staple here.
- **13:00** — Lunch, rest.
- **16:00** — By boat on the **Rupununi River** to a pond of
  ***Victoria amazonica*** — the giant water lily, with pads up to
  three metres across that will hold a child's weight. **The
  flowers open white at dusk, are pollinated by a beetle they trap
  overnight, and reopen pink the next evening.** Timing the boat
  for that is the evening's point.
- **18:30** — **Arapaima** — the largest freshwater scaled fish in
  the world, up to three metres — surface to breathe air in the
  ponds here, and the local communities run the conservation
  programme that brought them back.
- **20:00** — Dinner.

## Day 7 — Deeper south, or back north

- **Option A — south, 08:00.** **Dadanawa** or the deep Rupununi,
  where the ranches are vast and the ***vaqueros*** still work
  cattle on horseback. This is as remote as the country gets
  without a serious expedition.
- **Option B — Surama, 08:00.** A **Makushi village that owns and
  runs its own eco-lodge** — you stay in the community, climb
  **Surama Mountain** before dawn for the view over the savannah,
  and the money goes to the village directly. It is the best
  community tourism in the country.
- **13:00** — Lunch.
- **16:00** — A final savannah drive, or the river.
- **19:00** — Dinner under a sky with no settlement for a hundred
  kilometres in any direction.

## Day 8 — Back to Georgetown

- **09:00** — **Fly back** from Annai or Lethem, about an hour and
  a half, over the forest again.
- **11:30** — Georgetown. A shower and a change.
- **13:00** — Lunch, then the **Guyana National Museum** or the
  **Walter Roth Museum of Anthropology**, which is small and good
  on the nine Amerindian nations.
- **16:00** — The **Demerara** name on your rum bottle at home
  comes from the river here — a **distillery tour** or at least a
  tasting of **El Dorado**, which is made at Diamond on the
  Demerara and is among the best rum in the world.
- **19:30** — Last dinner.

## Day 9 — Fly home

- **Morning** — **Cheddi Jagan is 40 km out**, about an hour, and
  **most international departures are very late at night** — so
  the last day is usually free and the night is not. Arrange
  transport through the hotel.
- **If you have the day** — the **Demerara Harbour Bridge** and a
  trip up the **Essequibo** to **Fort Island**, with its 1743
  Dutch fort and church.
- **Last buy** — El Dorado 15-year, and Amerindian basketry from
  the Hibiscus craft centre.

## What to pack

- **Long sleeves and trousers, light coloured**, for the forest
  and the savannah — this is both sun and insect protection.
- **Serious repellent** and **malaria prophylaxis**.
- **Binoculars.** This is a birding country of the first rank and
  the trip is half-wasted without them.
- **A head torch**, and a spare. The lodges run generators for a
  few hours.
- **Dry bags** for every boat, and **rain gear** — it is a
  rainforest and the dry season is only relatively dry.
- **Cash in USD and GYD** for the interior.
- **UK-style Type G and US Type A plugs both occur**, 240V.

## FAQ

**Is Kaieteur really better than Iguazú or Victoria Falls?**
Different, and the case for it is solitude. It is **the largest
single-drop waterfall in the world by height and volume
combined** — 226 m in one plunge — and you stand at an unfenced
edge with perhaps a dozen people on the whole plateau. Iguazú and
Victoria are bigger spectacles with thousands of visitors and
walkways. If being alone with it matters, nothing compares.

**What if the flight is cancelled?**
It happens regularly, and weather is the reason. **Build a spare
day in Georgetown**, and book Kaieteur early in your trip rather
than on the last day so there is a second chance.

**Is Georgetown safe?**
It has a real crime problem and warrants genuine care — arranged
transport, a reputable hotel, nothing on display, and no walking
after dark. **The interior is completely different** and is very
safe. Treat them as two separate places, because they are.

**Will I see a jaguar?**
Possibly, which is more than most places can say — **Iwokrama has
the highest recorded jaguar density in the world** and the forest
road produces sightings. It is still luck. Giant anteater, giant
otter, black caiman and arapaima are all far more likely.

**When should I go?**
**Mid-September to mid-November** or **mid-February to late
April**, the two dry seasons. The falls are at their most
powerful in the wet, when flights are cancelled most often.
