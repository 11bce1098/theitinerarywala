Argentina is 3,700 km from top to bottom, and that is the planning
problem in one sentence. You cannot drive this trip. What you can do
is fly three times and see three completely different countries: a
European capital that eats late and dances later, subtropical
rainforest with the largest waterfall system on earth, and Patagonia,
where the ice comes down to the water.

**Buenos Aires** first, for the steak, the bookshops and a tango
scene that survives the tourist version. **Iguazú** next, seen from
the Argentine side where you walk out over the falls. Then south to
**El Calafate** for the Perito Moreno glacier, and **El Chaltén**,
which exists purely because people wanted to walk to Fitz Roy.

Twelve days is the minimum that does not feel like a series of
airports.

## Before you go: flights, money and the seasons

### Entry, and the season that flips

- **Most nationalities enter visa-free** for 90 days — the EU, UK,
  US, Canada, Australia and most of Latin America. Check your own
  case; Argentina has changed its reciprocity arrangements before.
- **The seasons are inverted.** Patagonia's usable window is
  **November to March**, its summer. Outside that, El Chaltén's
  trails and much of the infrastructure shut, daylight collapses and
  the wind becomes genuinely dangerous.
- **December to February** is peak: best weather, busiest trails,
  highest prices, and accommodation in El Chaltén booked months out.
- **Iguazú is subtropical and works year-round**, hot and humid in
  summer, pleasant in the shoulder months.
- The practical answer for most people is **March or November** —
  Patagonia still open, crowds thinner, prices lower.

### The money situation, honestly

- **Argentina's currency is unstable**, and that is not a criticism,
  it is a planning fact. Inflation has run high for years and the
  peso moves fast enough that a figure printed today may be wrong in
  a month. **This guide budgets in US dollars** for that reason.
- **Bring US dollars in cash, in clean large notes.** They remain
  the most useful thing in your bag, and many tourist businesses
  quote in them directly.
- **Card payments have historically settled at a tourist-favourable
  rate**, but the rules here have changed repeatedly. Check the
  current position close to travel rather than trusting any guide,
  including this one.
- **Never change money on the street.** Use your hotel, a formal
  exchange house, or your card.
- Prices in pesos will look enormous. Fix a conversion on arrival
  and re-check it mid-trip.

### Internal flights are the itinerary

- **Three flights make this trip**: Buenos Aires → Iguazú → Buenos
  Aires → El Calafate, then home from El Calafate or back via
  Buenos Aires.
- **Buenos Aires has two airports.** Most domestic flights use the
  city one, **Aeroparque (AEP)**; most international ones use
  **Ezeiza (EZE)**, 45 km away. Connections between them need at
  least three hours, and ideally a night.
- **Book domestic flights early.** Prices rise steeply and Patagonian
  routes fill in the summer.
- **El Calafate → El Chaltén is a three-hour bus**, comfortable and
  frequent. No flight needed.

### Meals, and the clock

- **Argentina eats late.** Restaurants fill at 22:00 and some do not
  open until 20:30. Arriving at 19:00 means eating alone.
- **The steak is the real thing**, and a *parrilla* is the place for
  it. Order it *jugoso* (rare) or expect well done.
- **Lunch menus (*menú del día*)** are the cheap way to eat well.
- Tipping is around 10% and often cash only.

## Day 1 — Land in Buenos Aires

- **Afternoon** — Land at Ezeiza, transfer in. Sleep in **Palermo**
  or **Recoleta**.
- **Evening** — Walk, eat late, adjust.

## Day 2 — The city's two halves

- **Morning** — **Recoleta Cemetery**, a city of marble mausoleums
  where Evita is buried.
- **Afternoon** — **San Telmo**'s cobbles and antiques, then
  **Plaza de Mayo** and the pink presidential palace.
- **Evening** — A *parrilla*, properly, and late.

## Day 3 — Tango, books and football

- **Morning** — **El Ateneo Grand Splendid**, a bookshop in a
  converted opera house.
- **Afternoon** — **La Boca**'s painted streets — touristy, brief,
  and best in daylight — or a football stadium tour.
- **Evening** — Tango. The dinner shows are for visitors; a
  *milonga* is where people actually dance.

## Day 4 — Fly north to Iguazú

- **Morning** — Fly to **Puerto Iguazú**, about two hours.
- **Afternoon** — Settle. The falls need a full day, not half of one.
- **Evening** — The Argentine side's national park closes at dusk;
  save it for tomorrow.

## Day 5 — Iguazú, the Argentine side

- **All day** — The **upper and lower circuits**, then the train to
  the **Devil's Throat** walkway, where you stand directly above the
  largest drop. Expect to get soaked and to stop caring.
- **Evening** — Back to town.

## Day 6 — The Brazilian side, or the jungle

- **Option A** — Cross to the **Brazilian side** for the panoramic
  view: the Argentine side puts you in the falls, the Brazilian side
  shows you their scale. Check whether your passport needs a
  Brazilian visa before planning this.
- **Option B** — Stay Argentine: a boat under the falls, or the
  jungle trails and birdlife.
- **Evening** — Fly or overnight back to Buenos Aires.

## Day 7 — South to Patagonia

- **Morning** — Fly Buenos Aires → **El Calafate**, about three
  hours. The landscape out the window changes completely.
- **Afternoon** — Arrive. The town is small, windy and geared
  entirely to the glacier.
- **Evening** — Book tomorrow early.

## Day 8 — Perito Moreno

- **All day** — The **glacier**: a 5 km wall of ice, 70 m high,
  which calves into the lake with a noise like artillery while you
  watch from steel walkways.
- **Optional** — The boat that takes you close to the face, or an
  ice trek on the glacier itself.
- **Evening** — Back to El Calafate.

## Day 9 — Bus to El Chaltén

- **Morning** — Three hours north by bus, with Fitz Roy appearing
  on the horizon for the last hour.
- **Afternoon** — An easy first walk — **Chorrillo del Salto** or
  the **Mirador de los Cóndores**.
- **Evening** — Eat early; the town starts hiking at dawn.

## Day 10 — Laguna de los Tres

- **All day** — The big one: about 20 km return to the lake below
  **Fitz Roy**, with a brutal final kilometre straight up. Eight to
  ten hours. Start early and check the wind forecast, which matters
  more than the rain here.
- **Alternative** — **Laguna Capri** for the same view at half the
  effort.

## Day 11 — El Chaltén, gentler

- **Morning** — **Laguna Torre**, flatter and quieter, with Cerro
  Torre at the end of it.
- **Afternoon** — Bus back to El Calafate.
- **Evening** — Last Patagonian lamb.

## Day 12 — Fly home

- Fly El Calafate → Buenos Aires, and connect. **Leave a long gap**
  if you are changing airports in Buenos Aires, or sleep there.

## What to pack

- **Wind protection above all** for Patagonia. It is relentless, and
  a windproof shell matters more than a warm coat.
- **Proper hiking boots** if you are doing Laguna de los Tres.
- **Layers for four seasons in a day** — Patagonian weather changes
  hourly.
- **A dry bag or poncho for Iguazú.** You will be soaked.
- **US dollars in cash**, clean and undamaged.
- **Type I plugs** (the Australian-style angled pins), 220V.

## FAQ

**Can I do this in a week?**
Not honestly. Cutting Iguazú makes a tidy 8-day Buenos Aires and
Patagonia trip; cutting Patagonia defeats the point. Twelve days is
the shape that works.

**Which side of Iguazú is better?**
Both, for different reasons — Argentina puts you *in* the falls,
Brazil shows their scale. If you only do one, do Argentina, which is
where this itinerary is based.

**Is the currency situation a problem for visitors?**
It is a planning complication, not a barrier. Bring dollars cash,
check the current card position before you fly, and never change
money on the street.

**When should I go?**
**November to March** for Patagonia, and **March or November** if
you want that window without the peak-season crowds and prices.
