Chile is 4,300 km long and never more than 350 km wide, which makes
it the most absurd country to plan and one of the most rewarding to
visit. This trip takes the two ends and skips almost everything
between, because the two ends are extraordinary and the middle is a
long drive.

**The Atacama** is the driest non-polar desert on earth — salt flats
with flamingos, geysers that erupt at dawn, and a night sky so clear
that the world's major observatories are built here. **Torres del
Paine** is the other end: granite towers over glacial lakes, guanacos
on the steppe, and wind that has its own reputation.

**Santiago** sits in between, worth two days rather than five.

Two internal flights make this work. Without them it is a three-week
trip.

## Before you go: altitude, bookings and the wind

### Entry, and the seasons at both ends

- **Most nationalities enter visa-free** for 90 days — the EU, UK,
  US, Canada, Australia and most of Latin America.
- **Chile's agricultural controls are strict and enforced.** Declare
  all food, seeds, wood and plant material on arrival; fines are
  substantial and the dogs are good.
- **The seasons are inverted, and the two ends disagree.**
  **Patagonia works November to March** and shuts down outside it.
  **The Atacama works year-round**, though January and February
  bring the "Bolivian winter" — brief afternoon storms that can
  close roads.
- **December to February** is Patagonian peak: best weather, most
  people, highest prices, refuges booked out.
- **November and March** are the sweet spot — the park open, the
  wind slightly less relentless, prices lower.

### The altitude catches people out

- **San Pedro de Atacama sits at 2,400 m**, and the excursions go
  much higher — the geysers at **4,300 m**, the altiplano lagoons
  above 4,000 m.
- **Do not do the geysers on your first morning.** Give yourself a
  day at San Pedro's altitude first. The guide below sequences it
  that way deliberately.
- Drink far more water than feels necessary; the humidity here is
  close to nil and you dehydrate without noticing.

### Torres del Paine needs booking months ahead

- **Park entry is now booked online in advance** and capped. Buy it
  before you arrive; it is not reliably available at the gate.
- **If you are trekking the W or the O circuit, the refuges and
  campsites sell out months ahead** — often by August for the
  following January. They are run by two separate concessions plus
  the park service, and you book each separately, which is the
  single most annoying part of planning Chile.
- **If you are not trekking**, day walks from a hotel outside the
  park need no booking beyond entry, and this itinerary assumes that.
- **The wind is the real weather.** It routinely exceeds 100 km/h in
  spring and can stop a walk. Check the forecast each morning and
  believe it.

### Money and getting around

- **The peso (CLP)**, and prices run to the tens of thousands. Fix a
  mental conversion on arrival.
- Cards work in cities and hotels. **Carry cash in San Pedro and in
  Patagonia**, where some operators and small places are cash only.
- **Internal flights** — Santiago to Calama for the Atacama, and
  Santiago to Punta Arenas for Patagonia — are the itinerary. Book
  early; they rise sharply.
- **Punta Arenas to Puerto Natales is a three-hour bus**, frequent
  and comfortable.
- **Hiring a car in Patagonia** gives freedom the buses do not,
  and is worth it if you are confident on gravel.

## Day 1 — Land in Santiago

- **Afternoon** — Land, drop bags in **Lastarria** or **Providencia**.
- **Evening** — Walk, eat. Chile eats earlier than Argentina but
  later than Europe.

## Day 2 — Santiago

- **Morning** — **Cerro Santa Lucía** or **San Cristóbal** for the
  view over a city with the Andes behind it.
- **Afternoon** — The **Pre-Columbian Art Museum**, which is small
  and excellent, and the **Central Market** for lunch.
- **Evening** — **Bellavista** for dinner, or the wine bars of
  Lastarria.

## Day 3 — North to the desert

- **Morning** — Fly to **Calama**, two hours, then an hour by road
  to **San Pedro de Atacama**.
- **Afternoon** — **Do nothing strenuous.** You are at 2,400 m and
  tomorrow goes higher. Walk the adobe village.
- **Evening** — **Valle de la Luna** for sunset over salt-crusted
  badlands.

## Day 4 — Salt flats and flamingos

- **Morning** — **Laguna Chaxa** in the **Salar de Atacama**, where
  three flamingo species feed on a salt crust that cracks underfoot.
- **Afternoon** — The **altiplano lagoons** — Miscanti and Miñiques
  — at over 4,000 m, which is why today is not day one.
- **Evening** — Stargazing. The skies here are among the clearest on
  earth, and the small observatory tours are genuinely good.

## Day 5 — Geysers, at dawn

- **04:00** — **El Tatio**, the highest geyser field in the world at
  4,300 m. The eruptions are most dramatic in the cold before
  sunrise, which is why the start is brutal. It will be below
  freezing.
- **Late morning** — The hot springs on the way down.
- **Afternoon** — Sleep, or the **Valle del Arcoíris**.

## Day 6 — South, the long way

- **Morning** — Road to Calama, fly to **Santiago**.
- **Afternoon** — Connect on to **Punta Arenas**, or overnight in
  Santiago if the timings do not work. This is the one heavy travel
  day.
- **Evening** — Arrive at the bottom of the continent.

## Day 7 — To Puerto Natales

- **Morning** — Bus north, three hours, along the Strait of
  Magellan and out onto the steppe.
- **Afternoon** — **Puerto Natales**, a small port town on a fjord
  and the base for the park.
- **Evening** — Check your park booking, buy anything you have
  forgotten, and look at tomorrow's wind forecast.

## Day 8 — Into Torres del Paine

- **Morning** — Two hours into the park. The first sight of the
  **Cuernos** across Lago Pehoé is the one people remember.
- **Afternoon** — **Salto Grande** and the **Mirador Cuernos**, an
  easy walk with the best reward-to-effort ratio in the park.
- **Evening** — Sleep in or near the park.

## Day 9 — Base of the Towers

- **All day** — The **Mirador Las Torres**: about 18 km return, seven
  to nine hours, with a boulder field for the last kilometre and
  three granite towers over a green lake at the end. The park's
  signature walk, and a hard one.
- **Alternative** — **Lago Grey** and the glacier face by catamaran,
  for a fraction of the effort.

## Day 10 — Grey Glacier, or the French Valley

- **Option A** — The **Grey Glacier** boat, which takes you to the
  ice face and among the bergs.
- **Option B** — Guanacos and condors on the eastern steppe, which
  is where the wildlife actually is.
- **Evening** — Back to Puerto Natales.

## Day 11 — South, and the penguins

- **Morning** — Bus to **Punta Arenas**.
- **Afternoon** — **Magdalena Island**'s penguin colony in season
  (roughly November to March), or the **Nao Victoria** museum and
  the cemetery, which is stranger and better than it sounds.
- **Evening** — Last Patagonian dinner.

## Day 12 — Fly home

- Fly Punta Arenas → Santiago and connect. Leave a long gap;
  Patagonian weather delays flights and the connection is the whole
  length of the country.

## What to pack

- **Serious wind protection** for Patagonia. A windproof shell
  matters more than insulation.
- **Layers for freezing dawns and warm afternoons**, at both ends —
  the desert at 4,300 m is below zero at 6am and pleasant by ten.
- **Proper boots** if you are walking to the Towers.
- **Extreme sun protection.** Thin air and thin ozone at both ends.
- **Type C/L plugs**, 220V.
- **Cash in pesos** for San Pedro and Patagonia.

## FAQ

**Can I do this without the internal flights?**
Not in twelve days. Santiago to Punta Arenas is over 3,000 km; the
bus takes more than a day and a half.

**Do I need to trek the W circuit?**
No. This itinerary does day walks from a base, which gets you the
Towers, the Cuernos and the glacier without booking refuges a year
out. The W is a fine trip and a different one.

**Should I combine it with Argentina?**
It is the natural pairing — El Calafate is a few hours from Puerto
Natales across the border, and many people do both. See our
[Argentina guide](/itineraries/argentina-12-days-guide/) for that
half.

**When should I go?**
**November to March** for Patagonia, with **November and March**
giving the best balance of open trails, lower prices and marginally
calmer wind.
