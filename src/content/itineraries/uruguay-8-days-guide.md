Uruguay is the country people pass over on the way between Buenos
Aires and Rio, and it is the calmest place on the continent. Three
and a half million people, half of them in one city, and a
coastline that goes from a 22-kilometre urban promenade to a
village you can only reach by four-wheel-drive over dunes.

**Colonia del Sacramento** was founded by the Portuguese in 1680
to smuggle goods past the Spanish, changed hands nine times, and
its cobbled quarter is a UNESCO site **one hour by ferry from
Buenos Aires** — which is why this trip attaches so naturally to
Argentina.

At the other end, **Cabo Polonio** has **no road, no mains
electricity and no street lighting**, a sea lion colony under the
lighthouse, and one of the darkest skies you can drive to.

It is also, quietly, expensive — and there is one refund that
makes a real difference.

## Before you go: the car, the refund and the season

### Getting in, and getting around

- **Carrasco (MVD)** is 20 km east of Montevideo and small.
- **Many people arrive from Buenos Aires** instead: the
  **Buquebus ferry** reaches **Colonia in about an hour** and
  Montevideo in two and a quarter, which makes this an easy add-on
  to an Argentina trip. See our
  [Argentina guide](/itineraries/argentina-12-days-guide/).
- **Hire a car.** The east coast beyond Punta del Este is not
  usefully served by bus, and the whole point of the second half of
  this trip is the ability to stop. Roads are good, traffic is
  light, and distances are short.
- **Buses** are comfortable and frequent between the main towns if
  you would rather not drive — Montevideo to Punta del Este is two
  and a half hours.

### The refund nobody tells you about

- **Foreign visitors get the 22% VAT refunded automatically** on
  restaurant meals, and on hotel stays, **when you pay with a
  non-Uruguayan card**. It is deducted at the point of sale — no
  forms, no airport desk.
- This is not a small thing in a country this expensive. **Pay
  with a foreign card rather than cash**, which gets no discount.
- Hotels additionally charge **no VAT at all** to non-residents
  paying by foreign card, provided you show your passport at
  check-in.

### The season swings hard

- **December to February** is summer and the whole country goes to
  the coast. **Punta del Este is genuinely packed**, prices double
  or triple, and booking is essential.
- **March is the best month**: the water is still warm, the crowds
  have gone back to work, and prices fall off a cliff after
  Carnival.
- **April and November** are quiet and pleasant with cooler sea.
- **June to August** is winter — cold, grey, wet, and much of the
  coast simply shuts. Cabo Polonio and Punta del Diablo are close
  to empty.

### Money and practicalities

- **Uruguayan peso (UYU).** Cards are widely accepted and
  preferred — see the refund above. ATMs dispense pesos and
  sometimes US dollars.
- **Mate is everywhere.** People walk with a thermos under one arm
  and a gourd in the other hand, all day, including at work. You
  will be offered it; accept, drink it all, hand it back.
- **Tap water is safe** across the country.
- Uruguay is **the safest country in South America** by most
  measures, with the ordinary caution of any city applying in
  Montevideo after dark.

## Day 1 — Land in Montevideo

- **13:00** — Land at **Carrasco (MVD)**, which is 20 minutes from
  the city and architecturally rather good for an airport.
- **14:30** — Stay in **Ciudad Vieja** for the old quarter or
  **Pocitos** for the beach and the restaurants. Pocitos is where
  Montevideans actually live.
- **16:00** — The **Rambla**: **22 kilometres of continuous
  seafront promenade**, said to be the longest in the world, and
  the spine of the city's daily life. Walk any stretch of it in
  late afternoon and you will pass several hundred people with
  thermos flasks, drinking mate and watching the river.
- **18:00** — This is the **Río de la Plata**, not the Atlantic —
  **the widest river in the world**, brown with silt, and 100 km
  across at Montevideo.
- **19:30** — Dinner. A ***chivito***, the national sandwich:
  steak, ham, cheese, bacon, olives, egg and salad in a bun, and it
  arrives larger than you expect.

## Day 2 — Montevideo properly

- **09:30** — **Ciudad Vieja**, the old town on its peninsula.
  **Plaza Independencia** with the **Palacio Salvo** — a 1928
  tower that was briefly the tallest building in South America and
  looks like it was designed by someone having a vivid dream —
  and the Artigas mausoleum beneath the square.
- **11:00** — **Teatro Solís**, 1856, the country's opera house,
  by guided tour.
- **12:30** — The **Mercado del Puerto**: a wrought-iron market
  hall from 1868, now wall-to-wall ***parrillas*** — open grills
  loaded with beef, sweetbreads, chorizo and provolone. Sit at a
  counter, order *parrillada*, and have a *medio y medio*, the
  local half-sparkling-half-white.
- **15:00** — Walk back along the port. **Montevideo is a working
  container port** and makes no attempt to hide it.
- **16:30** — The **Museo Torres García** for the Uruguayan
  constructivist, or the **Museo del Carnaval** — Montevideo's
  carnival is **the longest in the world at forty days**, and
  *candombe* drumming on the street is its heart.
- **19:00** — **Candombe** drumming in **Barrio Sur** or
  **Palermo** on a Sunday evening: three drums, descended from
  enslaved Africans brought here, played walking down the middle of
  the road. It is a neighbourhood event, not a performance.
- **21:00** — Dinner late.

## Day 3 — Colonia del Sacramento

- **09:00** — Drive west, **about two and a half hours** on Route 1.
- **11:30** — **Colonia**, and the **Barrio Histórico** is small
  enough to walk in an hour and good enough to spend a day in.
  Founded by **Portugal in 1680** purely to smuggle goods across
  the river past Spanish Buenos Aires, it **changed hands nine
  times** before Uruguay existed — and the street surfaces tell you
  which power laid them, Portuguese cobbles draining down the
  middle, Spanish ones to the sides.
- **12:30** — Lunch on **Calle de los Suspiros**, the most
  photographed street in the country.
- **14:30** — The **lighthouse**, built into the ruins of a 17th-
  century convent, for the view over the red roofs and the brown
  river.
- **16:00** — Rent a **golf cart** or a bicycle, which is what
  everyone does, and run the riverfront out past the old bullring
  at Real de San Carlos.
- **18:00** — **Sunset over the water** from the rocks by the old
  gate, with **Buenos Aires 50 km away** across the estuary and
  visible as a smudge on a clear evening.
- **20:00** — Dinner, and stay the night. Colonia empties when the
  last ferry leaves and is at its best after that.

## Day 4 — East to Punta del Este

- **09:00** — The long leg: Colonia to **Punta del Este**, about
  four hours, skirting Montevideo.
- **11:00** — Stop at a ***bodega***. Uruguay's grape is
  **Tannat** — brought by Basque immigrants in the 1870s, almost
  extinct in its native south-west France, and it produces a dark,
  tannic, structured red that is now the national wine.
  **Bodega Bouza** outside Montevideo is the convenient one and
  does lunch.
- **14:30** — Arrive **Punta del Este**, a peninsula with the
  **Río de la Plata on one side and the Atlantic on the other** —
  locals call them *mansa* (tame) and *brava* (wild), and the
  water genuinely changes colour and temperature between them.
- **16:00** — ***La Mano***, the fingers of a hand emerging from
  the sand on Playa Brava — Mario Irarrázabal, 1982, and it is
  about drowning, which nobody photographing it seems to know.
- **18:00** — The **port** at feeding time, where **sea lions**
  gather for the fishing boats and are entirely unbothered by
  anybody.
- **20:30** — Dinner. **In January this town is Monaco**; in March
  it is a pleasant beach resort at a third of the price.

## Day 5 — The coast, east of the money

- **09:30** — **Casapueblo** at Punta Ballena, 20 minutes west —
  the home and studio of the painter **Carlos Páez Vilaró**, built
  by hand over 36 years with no plans and no straight lines, in
  white stucco poured down a cliff. It is part Gaudí, part
  Santorini, part nothing else at all, and the **sunset ceremony**
  with his recorded poem is unashamedly sentimental and works
  anyway.
- **12:00** — **Isla de Lobos** by boat from the port if the sea
  allows: **one of the largest South American sea lion colonies in
  the world**, around 200,000 animals, on an island two hours
  round trip.
- **15:00** — East to **José Ignacio**, 40 minutes — a fishing
  village of sand streets and a lighthouse that became the place
  the Punta crowd escaped to. Still low-rise, still mostly sand,
  and the restaurants are excellent.
- **17:00** — **La Barra** on the way, with the undulating bridge
  that everybody drives over slightly too fast.
- **19:30** — Dinner at José Ignacio, watching the light go over
  the Atlantic.

## Day 6 — Cabo Polonio

- **09:00** — Drive east on **Ruta 10**, about two hours, past
  **Laguna Garzón** and its circular bridge.
- **11:00** — At the **Cabo Polonio** park entrance you **leave
  the car** and get into a **four-wheel-drive truck with
  bench seats** that crosses 7 km of dune and beach. There is no
  road, and this is the only way in apart from walking.
- **12:00** — Arrive. **No mains electricity, no running water in
  most houses, no street lighting, no cars.** Perhaps eighty
  permanent residents, solar panels, wind turbines, candles and a
  single lighthouse.
- **13:00** — Lunch at one of the handful of places.
- **15:00** — The **sea lion colony** on the rocks beneath the
  **lighthouse** — hundreds of them, loud and close, and you walk
  right up to the edge of it.
- **17:00** — Climb the lighthouse, then walk the dunes between
  the two beaches.
- **19:30** — Dinner by candle and generator.
- **21:30** — **The sky.** No street lighting for many kilometres
  and nothing on the horizon but sea. This is the reason to stay
  the night rather than take the truck back, and almost everyone
  who day-trips it says afterwards that they should have stayed.

## Day 7 — Punta del Diablo, and west

- **08:30** — The truck back across the dunes.
- **10:00** — **Punta del Diablo**, 45 minutes further east — a
  fishing village turned surf and backpacker town, sand streets,
  wooden houses, and genuinely the end of the country: the
  Brazilian border is 40 km on.
- **11:30** — **Santa Teresa National Park** next door: a
  Portuguese-then-Spanish hilltop fortress from the 1760s, pine
  forest planted over dunes, and long empty Atlantic beaches.
- **13:30** — Lunch, and then turn around.
- **15:00** — The drive west, **about four and a half hours** to
  Montevideo, or three to Punta del Este if you would rather break
  it.
- **19:30** — Last dinner. **Asado** if you can find somewhere
  doing it properly — Uruguay eats more beef per head than any
  country on earth and does not consider this a competition with
  Argentina, because it has already won.

## Day 8 — Fly home

- **Morning** — Carrasco is 20 minutes from the city.
- **Return the car** with time for the inspection and check the
  fuel policy.
- **Last stop** — **Tannat** to carry home, and *dulce de leche*,
  which Uruguay and Argentina both claim and which you can settle
  for yourself.

## What to pack

- **Layers.** The coast is windy year-round and evenings cool
  sharply even in summer.
- **A torch** for Cabo Polonio, which has no street lighting at
  all — this is not optional.
- **Cash for Cabo Polonio**, where card machines mostly do not
  work.
- **Sun protection.** The ozone layer over the southern cone is
  thin and the UV index is high even on cool days.
- **Swimming things and a windbreak** — Atlantic beaches here are
  beautiful and rarely still.
- **Type C/F/L plugs**, 230V.
- **Your passport at hotel check-in** to get the tax exemption.

## FAQ

**Can I do this from Buenos Aires?**
Yes, and many people should. The **ferry to Colonia is an hour**
and to Montevideo about two and a quarter. Three days gets you
Colonia and Montevideo comfortably; this full week adds the east
coast, which is the half most visitors never see.

**Is Punta del Este worth it?**
In January it is a crowded, expensive scene. **Outside peak it is
a genuinely good beach town** with excellent restaurants and
Casapueblo nearby. If you are there for the coast rather than the
scene, **José Ignacio** is the better base.

**Should I stay the night in Cabo Polonio?**
Yes. The day trip gives you sea lions and dunes; the night gives
you the stars with no electric light anywhere, which is the actual
experience. Accommodation is basic — a bed, a candle, a shared
bathroom — and that is the point.

**When should I go?**
**March**, if you can. Warm sea, empty beaches, and prices that
have collapsed since Carnival. December to February if you want
the coast at full volume; avoid June to August entirely unless
Montevideo alone is the plan.

**Is it really the safest country in South America?**
By most measures yes, and the difference from the region is
noticeable. Montevideo has normal urban crime and the usual
caution applies after dark in Ciudad Vieja; the coast and the
interior are very quiet indeed.
