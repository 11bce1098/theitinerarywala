Three capitals sit within four hours of each other by train, and the
railway between them is one of the reasons Europe is worth the
paperwork. **Prague** for the storybook version — a castle on a hill,
a bridge lined with saints, a square where the clock draws a crowd on
the hour. **Vienna** for the imperial one — Habsburg palaces, a
coffee-house culture that treats sitting down as a discipline, and
concert halls that still sound the way they were designed to.
**Budapest** for the one people remember longest — a parliament
building that looks staged, and a city that solved winter by building
swimming pools on top of its hot springs.

Ten days is enough for three nights in each, with two travel days
that are themselves worth having. Book it open-jaw — **into Prague,
home from Budapest** — and you never backtrack.

The catch is the small print: **three countries, three currencies**,
and only one of them uses the euro.

## Before you go: one visa, three currencies

### One Schengen visa covers all three

- **All three are in the Schengen area**, so one visa covers the
  trip. Apply to the country where you will spend the most nights —
  on this route that is a tie at three each, which means **you apply
  to the country you enter first**: the Czech Republic. The full
  process, the lead times and the documents that actually cause
  refusals are in our
  [Schengen visa guide](/guides/schengen-visa-how-it-works/).
- Visa-free nationalities need nothing beyond a passport, but the
  **EES biometric border** has been fully operational since April
  2026 — allow extra queue time on your first arrival.
- **Book flights open-jaw** (into Prague, out of Budapest). A return
  into one city costs you a wasted day going back for it.

### Three currencies, and the one trap

- **Czech Republic: the koruna (CZK)**, not the euro. Prices quoted
  in euros on a tourist-strip menu are a markup, not a convenience.
- **Austria: the euro.** The only one.
- **Hungary: the forint (HUF)**, not the euro either, whatever the
  menus near the river imply.
- **The exchange-office warning is real in Prague**, and the same
  trick appears in Budapest: storefronts advertising a great rate,
  with the real one applied after a "commission". Use bank-branded
  ATMs, decline the terminal's offer to charge you in your home
  currency, and walk past the blue Euronet machines in all three
  cities.
- Cards tap everywhere in all three capitals. Carry a small amount of
  local cash for markets, cloakrooms and the occasional bath locker.

### The trains are the trip

- **Prague → Vienna**, roughly **4 hours**, and **Vienna →
  Budapest**, roughly **2 hours 40**. Both run several times a day on
  comfortable intercity services.
- **Book the long legs in advance.** These operators release cheap
  advance fares two to three months out and raise them as seats sell
  — the same pattern as everywhere else in Europe, and a bigger
  saving than any pass delivers on a route this short. Our
  [rail pass guide](/guides/rail-passes-and-getting-around/) explains
  why a pass is the wrong instrument for three long legs.
- **No rail pass needed** for this route. Price the two journeys
  point-to-point; the arithmetic will not be close.
- Seat reservations are worth taking on the Prague–Vienna leg in
  summer, when it fills.

### The habits each city expects

- **Vienna's transport runs on trust** — no barriers on the U-Bahn,
  and inspectors who are not charmed by tourists. Validate your
  ticket.
- **Sundays close Austria.** Shops shut; museums, cafés and bakeries
  stay open. Plan Vienna's Sunday as a museum-and-coffee day and you
  will not notice.
- **The Viennese coffee house is a place to sit, not to queue.** You
  order, you stay, nobody hurries you, and asking for "a coffee" will
  get you a raised eyebrow — name one.
- **Budapest's baths have their own etiquette**: bring flip-flops and
  a towel, rent a cabin rather than a locker if you want somewhere to
  change in peace, and check which pools are swim-cap-only.

## Day 1 — Land in Prague, meet the Old Town

- **14:00** — Land at **Václav Havel (PRG)**. There is no train:
  the **119 bus to Nádraží Veleslavín** and then metro line A is
  the 35-minute answer, and a single ticket covers both.
- **15:30** — Drop bags. Stay in the **Lesser Town (Malá Strana)**
  or **Vinohrady** rather than on the Old Town Square, where you
  pay double to be woken by stag parties.
- **16:30** — Walk to the **Old Town Square**. The **astronomical
  clock** performs on the hour: twelve apostles pass two windows,
  a skeleton rings a bell, and it lasts **45 seconds**. The crowd's
  reaction is honestly half the spectacle.
- **17:30** — **Cross Charles Bridge heading west**, when the
  day-trip coaches have gone and the light is low on the castle.
  Thirty statues, and at six in the evening you can actually see
  them.
- **19:30** — Dinner in the **Lesser Town** below the castle, where
  the streets empty early and the prices are calmer.

## Day 2 — Castle hill, done before the queues

- **08:00** — **Be at Prague Castle when it opens.** It is a walled
  district rather than a building — the largest ancient castle
  complex in the world — and by eleven it is a slow-moving
  procession.
- **08:30** — **St Vitus Cathedral** first, for the Mucha window
  and the silver tomb, then the **Old Royal Palace**, **St George's
  Basilica** and **Golden Lane**.
- **11:00** — Walk **down through the vineyard** to the river
  rather than retracing your steps — there is a terrace café
  halfway with the best view in Prague and almost nobody on it.
- **13:00** — Lunch in Malá Strana.
- **14:30** — The **Jewish Quarter (Josefov)**: six synagogues and
  the **Old Jewish Cemetery** on one combined ticket, where 12,000
  stones lean together over twelve layers of burials because the
  community was never permitted more land. The **Pinkas
  Synagogue's** walls carry the names of 78,000 Czech Jews
  murdered in the Holocaust, handwritten. It is the most affecting
  hour in the city.
- **19:00** — A **beer hall**. Czech beer is often **cheaper than
  water on the same menu**, the pour is taken seriously, and the
  food is built around it.

## Day 3 — Prague at your own pace

- **A free day, deliberately.** Three that reward it:
- **Option A — Vyšehrad, 10:00.** The other castle, on a cliff
  south of the centre: ramparts, a neo-Gothic church, the cemetery
  where Dvořák and Smetana are buried, and the view down the river
  **without the crowd**.
- **Option B — Petřín, 10:00.** The funicular up the hill for the
  lookout tower (a small Eiffel, which you climb), the mirror maze,
  and the rose gardens.
- **Option C — Kutná Hora, 09:00.** An hour each way by train, for
  the **Sedlec Ossuary** — a chapel decorated with the bones of
  40,000 people, including a chandelier using every bone in the
  human body — and St Barbara's Cathedral, which is the better
  building and gets a tenth of the visitors.
- **19:00** — Dinner, then **pack**. The train south is a morning
  departure and you want to be on it.

## Day 4 — The train to Vienna

- **09:00** — **Prague → Vienna**, about **four hours** on a
  direct RegioJet or ČD service. Book a seat; it is cheap and they
  fill. **Sit on the right leaving Prague** for the Vltava.
- **10:30** — The restaurant car is real, and the countryside
  earns the window seat.
- **13:00** — **Wien Hauptbahnhof**. Drop bags; the U-Bahn is
  immediate and faultless.
- **15:00** — Walk the **Ringstrasse** loop — the boulevard built
  on the demolished city walls in the 1860s, with the **opera**,
  **parliament**, **Rathaus** and the twin museums strung along it,
  each one built to be looked at rather than used.
- **17:30** — **Stephansdom** at dusk, and the tiled roof.
- **19:30** — Dinner in the lanes behind it. *Schnitzel* if you
  must, but **Tafelspitz** is the actual Viennese dish.

## Day 5 — Habsburg Vienna

- **08:30** — **Schönbrunn**, early. The **state rooms** fill from
  mid-morning and entry is timed, so book the first slot. The
  **Grand Tour** (40 rooms) is the one worth having.
- **11:00** — The **gardens behind are free** and worth an hour —
  up to the **Gloriette** for the view back down over the palace
  and the city.
- **13:00** — Lunch, then back into the centre.
- **15:00** — **Either** the **Hofburg** — the winter palace, with
  the Imperial Apartments and the Sisi Museum — **or** the
  **Kunsthistorisches Museum**, which holds **the largest Bruegel
  collection in the world**, including the Hunters in the Snow.
  If you have had enough palaces, take the Bruegels.
- **19:00** — **A concert.** Vienna sells a great deal of
  costumed tourist Mozart. The real thing is at the
  **Musikverein** or the **Staatsoper** — and **standing tickets at
  the opera cost less than a pizza**, sold from a separate door
  about 80 minutes before curtain. Queue, and take it.

## Day 6 — Coffee, cake and the other Vienna

- **09:30** — A **proper coffee house**, unhurried, with a
  newspaper on a wooden holder. **Café Central** is the famous one
  and the queue reflects it; **Sperl**, **Hawelka** and **Prückel**
  are better and emptier. Order a *Melange* and a *Sachertorte*,
  and **do not rush** — occupying a table for two hours is the
  entire point and the waiter expects it.
- **12:00** — The **Naschmarkt** for lunch — 120 stalls, and the
  Saturday flea market at the far end if your timing lands.
- **14:30** — The **Belvedere**: Klimt's ***The Kiss***, which is
  **smaller and considerably better** than every poster of it, plus
  the best Schiele collection anywhere, in a baroque palace with
  gardens between its two halves.
- **17:00** — The **Hundertwasserhaus** if you want the opposite
  of everything else — a council block with no straight lines and
  trees growing out of the windows.
- **19:30** — Dinner, and pack for the short hop east.

## Day 7 — The train to Budapest, and the river at night

- **09:30** — **Vienna → Budapest**, **under three hours** on a
  Railjet.
- **12:30** — Arrive at **Keleti**. Drop bags on the **Pest** side,
  which is the flat half where you will spend most evenings.
- **15:00** — Walk the **Danube promenade**: **Parliament** from
  the outside — the largest building in Hungary, built when the
  country was three times its current size — and then the **Shoes
  on the Danube Bank**, sixty pairs of iron shoes at the water's
  edge where Arrow Cross militia shot Jews into the river in
  1944–45 after making them undress. It takes a minute to walk past
  and you should not.
- **18:00** — Dinner early.
- **20:30** — **The best hour of the trip**: a **night cruise**, or
  simply walk across the **Chain Bridge**, with Parliament and the
  castle lit from the water. Budapest is better at night than
  either of the other two capitals.

## Day 8 — Buda hill, and the baths

- **08:00** — **Castle Hill**. The **Fisherman's Bastion** turrets
  are **free before 9am** — after that the upper terraces are
  ticketed — and the view across to Parliament is the shot everyone
  comes for.
- **09:30** — **Matthias Church** with its patterned tile roof, and
  the castle district's quiet back streets.
- **12:00** — Down, and lunch.
- **14:00** — **The baths**, and give them three hours minimum.
  **Széchenyi** is the big neo-baroque outdoor one, where men play
  chess on floating boards in the steam at 38°C with snow on the
  ground. **Gellért** is the art-nouveau indoor one — tiles,
  stained glass, columns — and calmer. **Bring flip-flops, a towel
  and a swimming cap** for the lap pools, or pay for them there.
- **19:30** — Dinner.
- **21:00** — A **ruin bar** in the old Jewish quarter —
  **Szimpla Kert** is the original, a derelict block filled with
  junk furniture and a Trabant you can sit in. More interesting
  than its stag-do reputation.

## Day 9 — Budapest at your pace

- **Option A — Parliament, 09:00.** The interior tour, **booked
  well ahead** because it sells out, for the Crown of St Stephen
  and a staircase under 40 kg of gold leaf.
- **Option B — the 20th century, 10:00.** The **House of Terror**
  on Andrássy — the headquarters of both the Arrow Cross and then
  the communist secret police, in the same building, which is the
  point it makes.
- **Option C — food, 10:00.** The **Great Market Hall** for
  paprika, salami and *lángos* upstairs, then Andrássy to Heroes'
  Square.
- **Option D — out, 09:00.** The train up the **Danube Bend** to
  **Szentendre**, 40 minutes, for the baroque riverside town.
- **19:30** — Last dinner. **Hungarian food is heavier than the
  other two capitals, better than either, and roughly half the
  price** — goulash is a soup here, and *pörkölt* is the stew you
  were thinking of.

## Day 10 — Fly home from Budapest

- **Morning** — A slow one. Budapest's **Ferenc Liszt (BUD)** is
  small and calm by European standards; the **100E** express bus
  from Deák Ferenc tér takes 40 minutes.
- **Note** — Three countries, **three currencies**: Czech koruna,
  euros in Austria, Hungarian forint. **None of the three is
  useful in the next one**, so spend down as you go rather than
  carrying a pocket of dead notes home.

## What to pack

- **European Type C/F plugs** in all three — one adapter covers the
  whole trip.
- **Shoes for cobbles.** All three old towns are paved in them, and
  Prague's castle hill is a climb.
- **Swimwear, flip-flops and a quick-dry towel** for Budapest. Most
  baths rent towels, none of them cheaply.
- **A layer more than you think** between October and April. These
  are continental cities: colder than the coast, and drier.

## FAQ

**Is ten days enough for three capitals?**
For three nights each, yes, and that is the right amount. Two nights
per city means you spend the trip packing; four means you run out of
things you actually wanted to see.

**Which direction — Prague first or Budapest first?**
Prague first, as written. It is the gentlest introduction, the
trains run downhill in cost as you go east, and ending in Budapest
means ending on the baths.

**Could I add a fourth city?**
Bratislava sits an hour from Vienna and is a common add. It is a
pleasant half-day, not a stop worth a night on a trip this length.

**Do I need three sets of cash?**
Cards cover almost everything. Take a small amount of koruna and
forint from bank ATMs on arrival in each, and do not change money at
a storefront in either city.
