Belize is the size of Wales with 400,000 people in it, and it does
two things better than anywhere nearby.

Off the coast lies the **Belize Barrier Reef** — the **second
largest in the world** after Australia's, a UNESCO site, and close
enough to swim to from some beaches. Inland, under the limestone
of the **Cayo**, is **Actun Tunichil Muknal**: a cave the Maya used
for sacrifice during the drought that ended the Classic period,
where the skeletons and pots lie exactly where they were left
around 900 AD and you walk past them in socks.

It is also the one country in Central America where **English is
the official language**, and the one where the currency is **fixed
at two to the US dollar**, which removes most of the friction from
a short trip.

## Before you go: the cave, the reef and the seasons

### Entry, money and language

- **Most nationalities enter visa-free** for 30 days. There is a
  **departure tax**, usually bundled into your air ticket — check.
- **The Belize dollar is pegged at BZD 2 = USD 1**, fixed since
  1978. **US dollars are accepted everywhere**, often at exactly
  that rate, so there is very little to think about. Carry small
  notes; change is given in a mix of both.
- **English is official.** Kriol is what you will hear spoken, and
  Spanish, Garifuna, Maya and Mennonite German are all current.
- Cards work in hotels and tour offices; the cayes, buses and
  roadside food are cash.

### Book the ATM cave before anything else

- **Actun Tunichil Muknal** admits a limited number of visitors a
  day in groups of eight with licensed guides only. It is a full
  day from San Ignacio.
- **Cameras and phones are banned inside, entirely.** A visitor
  dropped a camera on a 1,100-year-old skull in 2012 and the ban
  has been absolute since. Do not plan photographs; this is the one
  place the memory has to do.
- You wade and **swim a short section at the entrance**, scramble
  over rock in the river for 45 minutes, then climb into the dry
  upper chamber and go **barefoot in socks** across the formations.
- **Not possible if you are claustrophobic, cannot swim, or cannot
  manage uneven rock for several hours.** There is no shortened
  version.

### When to go

- **Late November to April** is the dry season: reliable sun,
  flat water, best visibility on the reef. **February to April**
  is the sweet spot.
- **June to November is hurricane season**, with September and
  October the peak. It also rains hardest then, the ATM cave
  closes when the river is high, and prices drop accordingly.
- **May** is hot and humid and quiet, and still mostly dry.
- **Sargassum** — floating seaweed — can affect east-facing
  beaches from roughly April to August. It does not reach the
  snorkelling sites.

### Getting around

- **Water taxis** run Belize City to **Caye Caulker** in about 45
  minutes and on to Ambergris.
- **Shuttles** are how everyone crosses the country — Belize City
  or the water-taxi terminal to San Ignacio is about two and a
  half hours, and shared shuttles cost a fraction of a private
  transfer.
- **Buses** are old US school buses, cheap, slow and entirely
  usable.
- **Spend as little time in Belize City as you can.** It is a
  transit point with a real crime problem, and nothing on this
  itinerary requires staying there.

## Day 1 — Land, and out to the caye

- **11:00** — Land at **Philip Goldson (BZE)**, 15 km from Belize
  City.
- **12:00** — Transfer straight to the **water taxi terminal** and
  take the next boat. Do not linger in the city.
- **13:30** — **Caye Caulker**, 45 minutes out, and the island's
  motto is painted on a sign at the dock: **GO SLOW**. There are
  **no cars** — golf carts, bicycles and bare feet — and three
  sand streets named Front, Middle and Back.
- **14:30** — Drop bags. Lunch at a dock bar.
- **16:00** — Walk north to **the Split**, the channel that
  Hurricane Hattie cut through the island in 1961, where everyone
  swims and the bar plays music into the evening.
- **18:00** — Sunset from the west side.
- **19:30** — Dinner. **Lobster** in season (June 15 to February
  15 — it is illegal outside it, and a restaurant offering it in
  April is lying), or grilled snapper with rice and beans cooked
  in coconut milk.

## Day 2 — The reef

- **08:30** — The **Hol Chan Marine Reserve** day trip, which is
  the single best thing in the water here. Hol Chan is a natural
  cut through the reef, 9 m deep, and the current funnels fish
  through it in quantity.
- **09:30** — Snorkelling the cut: nurse sharks, green moray eels,
  enormous groupers, turtles, and spotted eagle rays. The reserve
  has been protected since 1987 and the difference from
  unprotected water is obvious within a minute.
- **11:30** — **Shark Ray Alley**, where fishermen once cleaned
  their catch and nurse sharks learned to gather. You get into
  water four metres deep **with a dozen nurse sharks and southern
  stingrays around you**. They are harmless, they are also very
  much there, and the first thirty seconds take some resolve.
- **13:30** — A **manatee** stop or a mangrove channel on the way
  back, depending on the operator.
- **15:30** — Back to Caulker. Swim off the Split.
- **19:30** — Dinner, and a rum punch that will be stronger than
  it tastes.

## Day 3 — Caulker slow, or the Blue Hole

- **Option A — go slow, 09:00.** Which is the island's actual
  instruction. Kayak or paddleboard the lee shore, snorkel the
  coral gardens straight off a boat for half a day, or do nothing
  at all with a book and a hammock.
- **Option B — the Great Blue Hole, 06:00.** A 300 m circular
  sinkhole in the reef, **124 m deep**, formed as a limestone cave
  that flooded when the sea rose. It is a **full day, ten hours,
  and expensive**.
- **Be clear about which you are:** the Blue Hole is a **diver's
  site** — three dives, the second along a wall past stalactites at
  40 m — and it is genuinely extraordinary underwater. **As a
  snorkeller you see a dark blue circle from the surface and very
  little else**, and the two reef stops on the same trip are the
  better part of your day. Half of the people who do it as
  snorkellers come back disappointed, and the money would be
  better spent on another Hol Chan trip.
- **16:00** — Back, whichever you chose.
- **19:30** — Last island dinner.

## Day 4 — Inland to San Ignacio

- **08:00** — Water taxi back to the mainland, 45 minutes.
- **09:30** — Shared shuttle west to **San Ignacio**, about two
  and a half hours on the Western Highway, past the Belize Zoo and
  the Mennonite farming country around Spanish Lookout.
- **12:30** — Arrive. San Ignacio is a hill town on the **Macal
  River**, the base for everything in the Cayo, and the first
  place on this trip that feels like a working Belizean town
  rather than a tourist one.
- **13:30** — Lunch at the market if it is a market day — Saturday
  is the big one.
- **15:00** — **Cahal Pech**, a small Maya site on the ridge
  directly above the town, ten minutes' walk up. Early Preclassic,
  occupied from around 1200 BC, and almost always empty.
- **17:00** — The riverside, and **confirm tomorrow's ATM pickup**.
- **19:00** — Dinner on Burns Avenue.

## Day 5 — Actun Tunichil Muknal

- **07:30** — Pickup, then 45 minutes to the reserve and a
  **45-minute jungle walk with three river crossings** — thigh to
  waist deep — to the cave mouth.
- **09:30** — **You swim into the entrance**, a few strokes in cold
  clear water under an overhang, and then it is an hour of wading
  and scrambling up the stream passage in the dark with headlamps,
  sideways through gaps, over boulders.
- **11:00** — You climb into the **dry upper chamber**, **take off
  your shoes**, and continue in socks so that nothing is scraped.
- **11:30** — And then: **pottery left where it was placed**, many
  pieces deliberately broken to release what they held. **Fourteen
  sets of human remains**, including infants. The Maya came here
  during the prolonged drought of the 9th century, went deeper and
  deeper, and the offerings escalated.
- **12:00** — At the back, on a ledge, the ***Crystal Maiden*** —
  a complete skeleton of a young adult, calcified into the cave
  floor over twelve centuries so that the bones sparkle.
- **14:00** — Out, back down the river, and lunch.
- **17:00** — Back in San Ignacio, quiet.

## Day 6 — Xunantunich and the Cayo

- **08:30** — West to the **Mopan River** and the **hand-cranked
  cable ferry** — a platform pulled across by a man with a crank
  handle, free, carrying your vehicle and about four others.
- **09:00** — **Xunantunich**: **El Castillo** rises **40 m**,
  the second tallest structure in Belize, and you climb it for a
  view over the forest canopy into **Guatemala**, which is three
  kilometres away. The eastern frieze — astronomical figures in
  moulded stucco — is one of the finest in the Maya world.
- **11:30** — Howler monkeys in the trees around the plaza, which
  you will hear long before you see.
- **13:00** — Lunch in **San José Succotz** or back in town.
- **14:30** — **Choose one:** the **Barton Creek Cave** by canoe,
  paddled a kilometre into the dark past burial ledges; the
  **Rio Frio Cave** and **Rio On Pools** in the **Mountain Pine
  Ridge**; or **Caracol**, the largest Maya site in Belize and a
  rough two-hour drive each way — bigger than Xunantunich and far
  emptier, but it eats the day.
- **18:30** — Back.
- **19:30** — Dinner, and arrange the morning shuttle south.

## Day 7 — South to Hopkins, and the Garifuna coast

- **08:00** — Shuttle south to **Hopkins**, about two and a half
  hours via the Hummingbird Highway, which is the prettiest road
  in the country.
- **11:00** — **Hopkins** is a **Garifuna** village strung along a
  single sand road beside the sea. The Garifuna are descended from
  shipwrecked West Africans and Caribs, were deported to Central
  America by the British in 1797, and their language, dance and
  music are on **UNESCO's intangible heritage list**.
- **12:30** — Lunch — *hudut*, fish in coconut broth with mashed
  plantain, which is the dish to order here.
- **14:30** — A **drumming lesson** at one of the village centres.
  The Garifuna drum is played with the heel of the hand and it is
  harder than it looks; the sessions are run by the drummers
  themselves and the money stays in the village.
- **16:30** — The beach, which is empty, or a bike along the sand
  road.
- **18:30** — Sunset, and the last swim.
- **Alternative** — **Placencia** an hour further south has more
  restaurants, a better beach and far more visitors.
  **Cockscomb Basin**, half an hour inland, is **the world's first
  jaguar preserve** and a good half-day of waterfall trails,
  though you will not see a jaguar.

## Day 8 — Fly home

- **08:00** — Hopkins to **BZE** is about two and a half hours by
  road. A **domestic hop** from Dangriga or Placencia to the
  international airport takes 30 minutes and costs a fraction of
  what you would expect, which is worth it if your flight is early.
- **12:00** — At the airport. It is small and straightforward.
- **Note** — **Spend the Belize dollars**; they are worth nothing
  outside the country. Keep the USD.

## What to pack

- **Reef-safe sunscreen.** Several marine reserves require it and
  the reef needs it.
- **Your own mask and snorkel** if you have them — you will use
  them most days.
- **Shoes you can swim and scramble in** for the ATM cave — old
  trainers, not sandals — and **a spare pair of socks**, which is
  what you walk the dry chamber in.
- **A dry bag**, for the cave river and every boat.
- **Long sleeves and repellent** for the Cayo — sandflies on the
  coast are worse than mosquitoes.
- **US-style Type A/B plugs**, 110V, same as North America.
- **Small USD notes**, which spend exactly like local currency.

## FAQ

**Is the ATM cave as intense as it sounds?**
It involves a swim, two hours of wading and scrambling in a river
in the dark, and a climb into a chamber with human remains in it.
Fit people in their seventies do it; people who are claustrophobic
or cannot swim should not. There is no photography and no shorter
route, and almost everyone who goes calls it the best thing they
did in Central America.

**Caye Caulker or Ambergris Caye?**
**Caulker** is smaller, cheaper, has no cars and a backpacker
rhythm. **Ambergris (San Pedro)** is larger, busier, has
restaurants and nightlife and golf-cart traffic. Both reach the
same Hol Chan reserve. This itinerary uses Caulker; swap if you
want more at night.

**Is the Great Blue Hole worth it?**
**If you dive, yes.** If you snorkel, probably not — you see a
dark circle from the surface, and the two reef stops on the same
trip are what you actually enjoy. It is a long, expensive day
either way.

**Do I need a car?**
No. Shuttles and water taxis cover this entire route, and in the
Cayo every tour includes transport.

**Is Belize safe?**
The country is fine for this itinerary with ordinary care. **Belize
City has a genuine violent crime problem** and is the one place to
transit rather than explore — which is why this guide moves you
through it in under an hour in each direction.
