Colombia is the most improved travel destination of the last twenty
years, and it is still catching up with its own reputation. What you
find now is three completely different countries inside one: the
**Caribbean coast**, tropical and Spanish-colonial; the **coffee
axis**, green valleys of palm-lined ridges; and the **Andean
cities** of Medellín and Bogotá, sitting at very different heights.

That last point matters more than it sounds. Cartagena is at sea
level, Medellín at 1,500 m, Bogotá at **2,640 m** — higher than
almost anywhere in Europe. This itinerary climbs gradually rather
than dropping you at the top.

Eleven days, three internal flights, and one bus leg that is worth
taking rather than flying.

## Before you go: altitude, flights and the honest safety brief

### Entry, and the climb

- **Most nationalities enter visa-free** for 90 days — the EU, UK,
  US, Canada, Australia and most of Latin America. You may be asked
  for onward travel.
- **Proof of yellow fever vaccination** is required for some
  arrivals and recommended for parts of the country. Check for your
  route.
- **Colombia now requires an online entry form** (Check-Mig) before
  arrival and departure. It is free and takes minutes; do it the day
  before each flight.
- **The altitude order is deliberate.** Cartagena at sea level,
  then the coffee region at around 1,900 m, Medellín at 1,500 m,
  and Bogotá at 2,640 m last. Bogotá first is what makes people
  feel rotten on day two.

### The safety brief, plainly

Colombia's reputation lags its reality by about fifteen years, but
"much safer than it was" is not the same as "no precautions".

- **"No dar papaya"** is the local phrase, and the whole philosophy:
  do not give anyone an easy opportunity. No phone out on a quiet
  street, no visible jewellery, no bag left on a chair back.
- **Use ride-hailing apps rather than hailing taxis**, especially in
  Bogotá and after dark.
- **Take what you need for the evening and leave the rest** in the
  hotel.
- **Stick to the areas visitors use** — Getsemaní and the walled
  city in Cartagena, El Poblado and Laureles in Medellín, La
  Candelaria and Chapinero in Bogotá — and take a car between
  neighbourhoods at night.
- **Do not accept drinks or cigarettes from strangers.** Scopolamine
  robbery is rare but real and is the one genuinely nasty risk.
- Outside the cities, this route is calm. Salento and the coffee
  valleys feel like a different country entirely.

### Flying beats driving

- **Colombia is mountainous and the roads are slow.** Bogotá to
  Medellín is 45 minutes by air and nine hours by road.
- **Book domestic flights early**; the low-cost carriers here are
  genuinely cheap when booked ahead and painful at the gate about
  baggage. Read the allowance before you pay.
- **The one drive worth taking** is Pereira or Armenia to **Salento**,
  about an hour, through the coffee valleys.
- Cartagena, Medellín, Pereira/Armenia and Bogotá all have airports
  on this route.

### Money and the small print

- **The peso (COP)**, and the numbers are large — a coffee runs into
  thousands. Fix a mental conversion on arrival.
- Cards work in cities and hotels; carry cash for markets, small
  restaurants and the coffee region.
- **Use bank-branded ATMs inside a bank or mall**, not street
  machines, and decline the terminal's home-currency conversion.
- **Tipping**: a 10% *propina voluntaria* is often added and you may
  decline it; most people pay it.
- Spanish is essential outside hotels. English is limited, and the
  effort is warmly received.

## Day 1 — Land in Cartagena

- **Afternoon** — Land, drop bags in the **walled city** or
  **Getsemaní**, which is livelier and cheaper.
- **Evening** — Walk the walls at sunset, then dinner in Getsemaní's
  plaza, where the street music happens.

## Day 2 — The walled city

- **Morning** — Early, before the heat: **Plaza Santo Domingo**,
  **Las Bóvedas**, and the balconied streets that are the whole
  point.
- **Afternoon** — **Castillo San Felipe**, the fortress, or the
  **Palace of the Inquisition** for the darker history.
- **Evening** — A rooftop for the sunset over the walls.

## Day 3 — Islands or mud

- **Option A** — **Islas del Rosario** for Caribbean water, a boat
  day.
- **Option B** — **Totumo mud volcano**, which is exactly as silly
  as it sounds and universally enjoyed.
- **Evening** — Pack; tomorrow you fly inland.

## Day 4 — Into the coffee region

- **Morning** — Fly to **Pereira** or **Armenia**, about an hour.
- **Afternoon** — Drive to **Salento**, an hour through the
  valleys. Bamboo, banana palms and steep green everywhere.
- **Evening** — The **Calle Real**'s painted shopfronts, and
  *trucha* — trout — which is the local dish.

## Day 5 — Cocora Valley

- **Morning** — **Valle de Cocora**, where **wax palms** — the
  tallest palms on earth, sixty metres — stand over cloud-forest
  ridges. The loop takes five to six hours; the shorter mirador
  walk gives the same photograph in ninety minutes.
- **Afternoon** — Back to Salento, and *tejo* — a bar game involving
  gunpowder — if you want the local evening.

## Day 6 — A coffee farm, properly

- **Morning** — A **finca tour**: picking, pulping, drying,
  roasting and cupping, explained by people who do it. The small
  family farms are better than the large show-farms.
- **Afternoon** — **Filandia**, quieter and prettier than Salento
  and twenty minutes away.
- **Evening** — Last valley night.

## Day 7 — Fly to Medellín

- **Morning** — Fly from Pereira or Armenia, under an hour.
- **Afternoon** — **El Poblado** or **Laureles**. The climate here
  is the reason it is called the city of eternal spring.
- **Evening** — Dinner in Provenza, which is where the city eats.

## Day 8 — Comuna 13 and the cable cars

- **Morning** — **Comuna 13** with a local guide: outdoor
  escalators, street art, and the honest story of what the
  neighbourhood was and how it changed. Go with a guide from there,
  not a bus tour.
- **Afternoon** — The **metrocable** up the hillside — public
  transport that doubles as the best view in the city — and
  **Parque Arví** at the top.
- **Evening** — **Plaza Botero**'s fat bronze sculptures, and the
  museum behind them.

## Day 9 — Guatapé

- **All day** — Two hours east: **El Peñol**, a 200-metre granite
  monolith with 700 steps up it and a lake of islands below, and
  **Guatapé** town, where every building carries painted
  *zócalos*.
- **Evening** — Back to Medellín.

## Day 10 — Fly to Bogotá

- **Morning** — Fly, 45 minutes. **You are now at 2,640 m** — take
  the first afternoon gently.
- **Afternoon** — **La Candelaria**, the colonial quarter, and the
  **Museo del Oro** — 55,000 pieces of pre-Columbian goldwork and
  the best museum in the country.
- **Evening** — Dinner in Chapinero or La Macarena.

## Day 11 — Monserrate, then home

- **Morning** — **Monserrate** by funicular for the view over a
  city of eight million in a bowl of mountains.
- **Afternoon** — Fly home.

## What to pack

- **Clothes for three climates.** Cartagena is hot and humid,
  Salento is spring-like and rains most afternoons, Bogotá is
  properly cool and needs a jacket.
- **Waterproof and walking shoes** for Cocora, which is muddy
  year-round.
- **Insect repellent** for the coast.
- **Type A/B plugs**, 110V.
- **A low-profile day bag**, in keeping with *no dar papaya*.

## FAQ

**Is Colombia safe for tourists now?**
On this route, yes, with ordinary urban caution applied
consistently. Millions visit annually. The phrase to remember is *no
dar papaya* — do not hand anyone an easy opportunity.

**Should I add the Caribbean coast beyond Cartagena?**
Tayrona and Santa Marta are excellent and add three days. They do
not fit inside eleven without cutting the coffee region, which would
be the wrong trade.

**Will the altitude bother me in Bogotá?**
Possibly, mildly, for a day — which is why it comes last here rather
than first. Take the first afternoon slowly and drink plenty of
water.

**When should I go?**
**December to March** and **July to August** are the drier windows.
Colombia sits on the equator, so it is less about temperature than
about rain, and the coffee region is green because it rains there
all year.
