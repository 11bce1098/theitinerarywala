Almost everyone goes to Peru for one photograph, and almost everyone
plans it in the wrong order. The mistake is the same every time:
land, fly straight to Cusco at **3,400 m**, and spend the first two
days of a hard-won trip with a headache.

This route does it the way the altitude wants. **Lima** at sea level
for two nights, which is no hardship — it is one of the great eating
cities. Then Cusco, but **straight down to the Sacred Valley** at
around 2,800 m to sleep, which is where you actually acclimatise.
Cusco itself comes later, when your body can enjoy it. **Machu
Picchu** sits in the middle, not at the end, so a cancelled train
does not end the trip.

The other thing to understand early: **almost nothing here is
bookable last-minute**. Permits, entry circuits and trains all sell
out, and the good hotels in Aguas Calientes go first.

## Before you go: the bookings that decide the trip

### Entry and the altitude

- **Most nationalities enter Peru visa-free** for 90 days on arrival
  — the EU, UK, US, Canada, Australia, most of Latin America and
  many others. Indian passports need a visa unless you hold a valid
  US, UK, Canadian, Schengen or Australian visa, which grants
  visa-free entry. Check your own case; the rules here change more
  often than most.
- **Altitude is the real planning constraint, not the paperwork.**
  Cusco is 3,400 m — higher than most people have ever slept. The
  Sacred Valley sits 500–600 m lower, and that difference is the
  reason this itinerary sleeps there first.
- Give yourself **two nights before any hard walking**, drink far
  more water than feels necessary, and go easy on alcohol the first
  two days. Coca tea is everywhere and helps a little; if you are
  prone to altitude sickness, ask a doctor about acetazolamide
  before you fly.

### The three things that sell out

- **Machu Picchu entry is timed and routed.** You book a specific
  circuit and a specific entry hour, and the daily total is capped.
  It sells out weeks ahead in high season and months ahead for the
  best slots. **Book this before your flights** if your dates are
  flexible at all.
- **The Inca Trail needs a permit**, capped per day including
  porters and guides, sold through licensed operators only, and it
  goes months in advance — often close to a year for peak dates.
  The trail also closes each February for maintenance. The
  **Salkantay** and **Lares** routes need no permit and are the
  usual fallback.
- **The train to Aguas Calientes** is the only practical way in
  without trekking, and it books out. Two operators run it; both
  sell online.

Verify all three on the official sites close to booking. Circuit
numbering and capacities have been revised more than once.

### Money, and the two-price system

- **The sol (PEN).** Cards work in Lima, Cusco and hotels
  everywhere; markets, taxis, small restaurants and every entrance
  in the Sacred Valley want cash.
- **US dollars are widely accepted but at a poor rate.** Pay in
  soles. Bring dollars only as a backup, and only clean notes.
- **The boleto turístico** is a combined ticket covering most Sacred
  Valley and Cusco sites. It is not optional at most of them — you
  cannot pay per site — so buy it on your first valley day.
- Withdraw from **bank-branded ATMs** and decline the machine's
  offer to charge you in your home currency.

### Getting around

- **Domestic flights** link Lima and Cusco in about ninety minutes,
  several times a day. Book the morning departure: Cusco's airport
  sits in a bowl and afternoon weather cancels flights.
- **A private driver for the valley day** costs little and saves
  hours of waiting. Agree the route and the price before setting off.
- **Taxis are unmetered.** Agree the fare first, or use a ride-hailing
  app in Lima, where they work properly.

## Day 1 — Land in Lima, sea level and dinner

- **Afternoon** — Land, drop bags in **Miraflores** or **Barranco**.
  The clifftop walk above the Pacific is the introduction.
- **Evening** — Eat. Lima is genuinely one of the world's great
  food cities, and it runs from a ceviche counter at a market to
  restaurants that book months out. Do both over two nights.

## Day 2 — Lima, and the reason to stop here

- **Morning** — **Museo Larco** for the pre-Columbian collection,
  which reframes everything you will see later, or the **Centro
  Histórico** for the colonial squares and catacombs.
- **Afternoon** — **Barranco**: the bohemian quarter, the bridge,
  and a slower pace than Miraflores.
- **Evening** — An early night. Tomorrow starts high.

## Day 3 — Fly to Cusco, then straight down to the valley

- **Morning** — Fly **Lima → Cusco**, about 90 minutes.
- **Immediately** — Do not stay in Cusco. Transfer straight to the
  **Sacred Valley** — Urubamba or Ollantaytambo — and sleep lower.
  This single decision is what makes the rest of the trip pleasant.
- **Afternoon** — Nothing strenuous. Walk the village, drink water,
  eat lightly.

## Day 4 — The Sacred Valley

- **Morning** — **Pisac**: the terraces above the town, and the
  market below it.
- **Afternoon** — **Ollantaytambo**, which is both a spectacular
  terraced site and the last living Inca town — the street plan is
  original.
- **Evening** — Early dinner in Ollantaytambo. The train leaves from
  here.

## Day 5 — Machu Picchu

- **Early** — **Train to Aguas Calientes**, then the shuttle bus up.
  Take the earliest slot you hold.
- **Your circuit** — Whichever you booked. The classic view comes
  from the upper terraces; the lower circuits get you closer to the
  stonework and are easier underfoot.
- **Optional** — **Huayna Picchu** or **Machu Picchu Mountain**, both
  separately ticketed, both capped, both booked at the same time as
  entry. Neither is for anyone uneasy with exposure.
- **Evening** — Sleep in Aguas Calientes, or train back to the
  valley if you would rather wake up somewhere nicer.

## Day 6 — Back to Cusco, now acclimatised

- **Morning** — Train and transfer up to **Cusco**, which by now
  will feel fine.
- **Afternoon** — **Qorikancha**, where a baroque convent was built
  straight onto Inca foundations and the joint is the point.
- **Evening** — **Plaza de Armas** at dusk, and dinner in San Blas.

## Day 7 — Cusco properly

- **Morning** — **Sacsayhuamán**, the fortress above town, walkable
  uphill if you are feeling strong and a short taxi if not.
- **Afternoon** — **San Pedro market**, the cathedral, and the
  artisan streets of San Blas.
- **Evening** — Free. Cusco's restaurants are better than a town this
  size has any right to.

## Day 8 — Rainbow Mountain, Maras, or rest

- Three honest options. **Maras and Moray** — the salt pans and the
  circular terraces — is the gentle one and the best value.
  **Rainbow Mountain** is a very early start and 5,000 m of altitude
  for a photograph. **Doing nothing in Cusco** is a legitimate third
  choice after a week at height.

## Day 9 — Fly home via Lima

- **Morning** — Fly Cusco → Lima. Take the earliest flight and leave
  a long connection; afternoon cancellations out of Cusco are common
  enough to plan around.

## What to pack

- **Layers, seriously.** The valley swings from cold mornings to
  strong sun to cold nights, all in one day, all year.
- **Proper walking shoes.** Inca stonework is uneven, polished and
  often wet.
- **Sun protection at altitude**, which burns far faster than the
  temperature suggests. Hat, high-factor sunscreen, lip balm.
- **Type A/B and C plugs** — Peru uses both, so bring a universal
  adapter.
- **A day pack with a rain layer** between November and March.
- **Cash in small notes** for site entries, markets and taxis.

## FAQ

**Is nine days enough?**
For this route, comfortably. Adding days is better spent on the
Amazon or Arequipa and Colca than on stretching this loop.

**Do I have to do the Inca Trail?**
No, and most people should not. It needs a permit booked many months
ahead, costs more than the train, and takes four days. Salkantay is
the better trek if you want one; the train is the better choice if
you do not.

**Will the altitude ruin it?**
Not if you sequence it as above. The people who suffer are almost
always the ones who flew into Cusco and went straight up to a
3,400 m hotel on day one.

**Which Machu Picchu circuit should I book?**
The upper circuits give the classic postcard view; the lower ones
bring you closer to the buildings. If the photograph is why you came,
book an upper circuit — and book it early, because those go first.

**When should I go?**
**May to September** is the dry season and the reliable choice.
January and February are wettest, and the Inca Trail closes each
February.
